# Research — The Emperor Who Won a War and Carved His Regret in Stone (stage 03)

TOPIC
New idea **#38** in `docs/ideas.md` (added in this session, out of the original 30): the Kalinga war (c. 261 BCE) and
Ashoka's Rock Edict 13, the one surviving royal account in which a conqueror counts the cost of his own victory. First
story from South Asia on the channel (region: India; epoch: Ancient World). Chosen as one of the two "real battle or
important event" stories the user asked for (2026-11-16). Popularity is **not measured**; Ashoka is widely known by
name, the edict's own words and the missing-in-Kalinga detail much less so.

RECOMMENDED STORY
Ashoka, third Mauryan king, conquers Kalinga (the east coast, today Odisha) in the eighth year after his coronation.
No account of the fighting survives; what survives is his own text, cut into rock: 150,000 deported, 100,000 killed,
"many more" dead of other causes, and "deep remorse". The edict says he is pained most by what befalls teachers,
ascetics and householders, not only soldiers; that "a hundredth, or even a thousandth part" of what happened at
Kalinga would now pain him; that even wrongdoers should be forgiven "where forgiveness is possible"; and in the same
text that the forest peoples are warned he still has the power to punish. The only conquest he now calls best is
"conquest by Dhamma", and he has the edict written so that his sons and great-grandsons will not make new conquests, or,
if they do, will use "forbearance and light punishment". The edict was cut at many places across the empire. In Kalinga
itself the edict is not there: Rock Edicts 13 and 14 are replaced at Dhauli and Jaugada by two separate edicts that
do not mention the war or any remorse, but address the officers of Tosali and Samapa. After Ashoka the script, Brahmi,
was forgotten; his memory lived in Buddhist legend; James Prinsep deciphered the script in 1837 and the king began to
speak for himself again.

STORY ANGLE
Not "the king who became good". A real text with real numbers, its own limits (he kept the army, he warned the forest
peoples), and the one detail that complicates it: the people who suffered the war never get the apology on their own
rocks. Left open, with the two scholarly explanations given once.

ONE-SENTENCE HOOK (verbatim into ideas.md and the script)
Around 261 BCE, an Indian emperor won a war, and then had his regret carved into rock.

HISTORICAL PERIOD
c. 261 BCE (eighth regnal year); edicts c. 260–256 BCE; decipherment 1837. For the site: **Ancient World**.

LOCATION / CULTURAL CONTEXT
Mauryan Empire, capital Pataliputra; Kalinga on the east coast (Odisha / northern Andhra Pradesh); edicts across the
empire from the north-west frontier (Shahbazgarhi, Kandahar) to the south (Erragudi).

STORY TYPE
Historical event / battle with a transformation: Situation → Objective → Conflict → Result → Consequences → afterlife
of the text.

KEY CHARACTERS
Ashoka (Devanampiya Piyadasi, "Beloved-of-the-Gods, He Who Looks On With Affection"); the people of Kalinga (no
individual named); James Prinsep.

CENTRAL STORY QUESTION
What does a conqueror say about his own victory — and who read it?

CORE CONFLICT
Empire against an independent neighbour; then the king against what he has done.

TURNING POINT
The edict: remorse in his own words, with its limits.

PAYOFF
The apology is on rocks everywhere except in the land that paid for it; and for two thousand years nobody could read it.

KEY EVENTS
1. **E** Rock Edict 13, in Dhammika's translation (*The Edicts of King Ashoka*, Buddhist Publication Society): "Beloved-
   of-the-Gods, King Piyadasi, conquered the Kalingas eight years after his coronation. One hundred and fifty thousand
   were deported, one hundred thousand were killed and many more died (from other causes) … Now Beloved-of-the-Gods
   feels deep remorse for having conquered the Kalingas."
2. **E** "Beloved-of-the-Gods is pained even more by this — that Brahmans, ascetics, and householders … who behave
   properly … are injured, killed or separated from their loved ones." "The killing, death or deportation of a
   hundredth, or even a thousandth part of those who died during the conquest of Kalinga now pains Beloved-of-the-Gods."
3. **E** "Even those who do wrong should be forgiven where forgiveness is possible." But: the forest peoples are told
   "that despite his remorse Beloved-of-the-Gods has the power to punish them if necessary."
4. **E** "Conquest by Dhamma … the best conquest"; envoys and effects reach Antiochos, Ptolemy, Antigonos, Magas,
   Alexander, and the Cholas and Pandyas in the south. "I have had this Dhamma edict written so that my sons and
   great-grandsons may not consider making new conquests, or that if military conquests are made, that they be done
   with forbearance and light punishment."
5. **E** Edict 13 is found at Erragudi, Girnar, Kalsi, Maneshra, Shahbazgarhi and Kandahar (Wikipedia, *Ashoka*), but
   "omitted from Ashoka's inscriptions found in the Kalinga region, where Rock Edicts 13 and 14 have been replaced with
   two separate edicts that make no mention of Ashoka's remorse" (Wikipedia). The Kalinga edicts in Dhammika: to the
   Mahamatras of Tosali — "All men are my children"; "some people are imprisoned, treated harshly and even killed
   without cause"; to those at Samapa — people beyond the borders should "live without fear of me".
6. **S** Why the omission: "one possibility is that Ashoka did not consider it politically appropriate to make such a
   confession to the people of Kalinga"; another, that the war and its consequences "are 'more imaginary than real'",
   a description meant "to impress those far removed from the scene" (Wikipedia, citing scholars). Both given once.
7. **S** Background of the war: Kalinga had ports and a navy and, per Megasthenes, an army of infantry, cavalry and
   elephants; "some scholars argue that Kalinga was a strategic threat" to the road between Pataliputra and the
   south (Wikipedia, *Kalinga War*). The fighting is "presumed" at Dhauli; no battle narrative exists.
8. **E** Afterlife: Brahmi forgotten; in Buddhist circles his image rested on legends (Ashokavadana), not on the rocks;
   Prinsep deciphered Brahmi in 1837; the identification of the Piyadasi of the rocks with Ashoka was confirmed later,
   by the Maski and Gujarra minor edicts that carry the name Asoka.

9. **E** Other edicts (Dhammika): RE 1 - "formerly, in the kitchen ... hundreds of thousands of animals were killed every
   day to make curry. But now ... only three creatures, two peacocks and a deer, and the deer not always"; RE 2 - medical
   treatment for humans and animals, "along roads I have had wells dug and trees planted"; RE 5 - new officers (Dhamma
   Mahamatras) appointed 13 years after the coronation, work for "proper treatment of prisoners, towards their
   unfettering" when a prisoner "has a family to support" or "is old"; RE 6 - reporters posted so that business reaches him
   "whether I am eating, in the women's quarters, the bed chamber, the chariot ...".
10. **S** Afterlife (Wikipedia, *Ashoka*): the Sultan Firuz Shah Tughlaq (14th c.) had pillars carried from Topra and Mirat
    to Delhi as trophies; per *Tarikh-i Firoz Shahi* Brahmin scholars told him the inscriptions foretold that nobody could
    remove them except a king named Firuz. The State Emblem of India is an adaptation of the Lion Capital of Ashoka.
11. **S** Legends (not in the edicts): Mahavamsa - he killed ninety-nine half-brothers, earlier called Chandashoka ("the
    Fierce"); scholars read 99 or 100 as an exaggeration meaning "several". Used once, marked as late legend.

CAUSES
Imperial expansion; the strategic value of the coast.

CONSEQUENCES
The end of Mauryan expansion in Ashoka's reign; a state ideology of Dhamma; envoys and edicts; a long period of peace
(Wikipedia: more than forty years).

MOST INTERESTING FACTS
- In nearly all the edicts the king never uses his own name, only "Beloved-of-the-Gods".
- He still has an army and still threatens; the edict does not renounce force.
- The apology is missing exactly where the war was fought.
- Dhammika: the edicts are "in his own words rather than in the stylistic language" of royal proclamations; "a distinctly
  personal tone … somewhat repetitious and plodding".

MYTHS / LEGENDS / TRADITIONS
Not in the script: the Ashokavadana stories of earlier cruelty ("Chandashoka") and of a conversion on the battlefield
or at a monk's word; the Sri Lankan tradition that he was already Buddhist in year 4. The edicts themselves say his
turn to Dhamma came *after* Kalinga and was gradual (the Minor Rock Edict: lay follower for 2.5 years before he
progressed). The script says only "after".

UNCERTAINTIES / DEBATES
1. The numbers are Ashoka's, not an independent count; the script says "his own figures" once.
2. Date: 261 BCE (Wikipedia, "probably"), 262 (Dhammika); "around 261".
3. Why it is missing in Kalinga: two explanations, no consensus.
4. Whether the war's cost is exaggerated ("more imaginary than real") — one scholarly view, mentioned with the omission.
5. Whether he was already a Buddhist before Kalinga (Eggermont) — **not used**.
6. "Dhauli hills as the battlefield" is presumed; **not used**.
7. The Daya river running red and other popular details — **not used**, no source.

VOICE CHARACTER
Calm, mature, exact. The numbers and the quoted words read plainly; no moralising.

VOICE DELIVERY
~143 wpm. A short beat after "carved into rock". Numbers one at a time, slowly. The quoted remorse in a lower, steadier
voice. A real pause before "In Kalinga itself". The last two sentences quiet and level.

WHY THIS VOICE FITS
The strongest thing in the story is a primary text. A commander voice would turn it into a victory speech; a dark one
would turn a sober document into melodrama.
