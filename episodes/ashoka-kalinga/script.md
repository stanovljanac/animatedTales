Around 261 BCE, an Indian emperor won a war, and then had his regret carved into rock.

The emperor was Ashoka, third king of the Mauryan dynasty, who ruled most of the subcontinent from the city of Pataliputra. Later Buddhist legends say that he reached the throne over the bodies of his brothers, and that he was so cruel that he was known as Ashoka the Fierce. Those legends were written down long after his time, and his own words say nothing of them. What his words do describe begins with a war.

On the eastern coast, in what is now Odisha, the kingdom of Kalinga was still independent. Some scholars think it threatened the Mauryan roads to the south, and it controlled the trade of the coast. Eight years after his coronation, Ashoka conquered it.

How the war was fought, nobody knows. No account of the battles has survived. What has survived is Ashoka's own account of the cost, cut into stone, in what scholars call the Thirteenth Rock Edict. One hundred and fifty thousand people were deported. One hundred thousand were killed. Many more died of other causes. These are his own figures, not an independent count.

And then, he says, something changed. After Kalinga was conquered, the king felt a strong inclination toward what he called Dhamma, a way of living by moral duty. Now, he says, he feels deep remorse for having conquered the Kalingas. The killing, the dying and the deportation pain him. But he says he is pained even more by the fate of the teachers, the ascetics and the householders who lived peacefully there, and who were injured, killed, or separated from the people they loved. Even a hundredth, or a thousandth, part of what happened at Kalinga would now pain him.

The other edicts show what that meant in practice. In the first, he writes that in his own kitchen, hundreds of thousands of animals used to be killed every day. Now only three are, two peacocks and a deer, and not always the deer. In the second, he says he has provided medical treatment for people and for animals, and has had wells dug and trees planted along the roads. In the sixth, he orders his reporters to bring him the people's business at any hour, whether he is eating, in the women's quarters, or in his carriage. And he appointed new officers, to see that prisoners were treated properly, and that those who had families to support, or who were old, were released.

It is not a renunciation of power, though. Ashoka kept his army. In the Thirteenth Edict he warns the forest peoples of his lands that, despite his remorse, he still has the power to punish them. What he gave up was conquest. The only victory he now called the best was a victory by Dhamma. He claimed that this kind of conquest had already reached further than any army: the edict names five Greek kings, Antiochos of Syria, Ptolemy of Egypt, Antigonos of Macedonia, Magas of Cyrene and Alexander of Epirus, and the Tamil kingdoms of the south. And he wrote that he had the edict carved so that his sons and great-grandsons would not start new wars, or, if they did, would show forbearance.

He had it cut in many places, on rocks and polished stone pillars across the empire, from the northwest frontier to the south. In Kalinga itself, it isn't there. At the sites in the conquered land, the rocks carry different edicts. They do not mention the war, or any regret. They speak to his officers, telling them that all men are his children, and that some people are being imprisoned, treated harshly and even killed without cause.

Why? Perhaps a king did not think it wise to confess to the people he had conquered. Perhaps, as some scholars suggest, the account of the war was written for people far away, who could not check it. Nobody knows.

Then the world forgot the writing itself. The script, Brahmi, went out of use, and for some two thousand years nobody could read it. Buddhist legends kept the memory of Ashoka, but not his words. In the fourteenth century, the sultan Firuz Shah Tughlaq had two of the pillars carried to Delhi. According to a chronicle of his court, he asked Brahmin scholars what they said, and they told him that the inscriptions foretold that only a king named Firuz could ever move them.

In 1837, the British scholar James Prinsep deciphered the script, and a king known mostly through stories began to speak for himself again. In most of his edicts, he does not even use his own name. He calls himself only Beloved of the Gods. Today, the lion carved on top of one of his pillars is the emblem of the Republic of India.

Of all the places where Ashoka said he was sorry, the one place where we do not find it is the land that paid for the war.
