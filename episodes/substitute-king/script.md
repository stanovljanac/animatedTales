When the moon went dark over ancient Assyria, the king's scholars had a way to save his life. They put another man on his throne — and when the danger had passed, they let that man die in the king's place.

To the people of Mesopotamia, the stars were the writing of heaven, and the scholars of the palace spent their nights reading it. An eclipse was the darkest message of all. They watched which part of the moon the shadow covered, because each part stood for a land. If the shadow fell on the king's land, the king would die.

But an omen was a warning, not a verdict. And a warning could be redirected.

In the seventh century BCE, Assyria was ruled by Esarhaddon. Hundreds of letters from his scholars survive, and they show how it was done.

When the shadow fell on his lands, Esarhaddon left his throne. Another man sat on it, washed, anointed and dressed in the king's robes, with a young woman beside him as his queen. One scholar wrote: "I wrote down whatever signs there were, and had them recited in front of Shamash," the sun god. "The substitute king took the signs on himself." Another bound the written omens into the hem of his robe.

The real king stayed hidden in his palace. His scholars kept writing to him, but no longer as king. Their letters begin: "To the farmer, my lord."

The substitute could sit on the throne for up to a hundred days. Then, in the words of the letters, he "went to his fate."

The scholars preferred a man whose death would trouble no one: a condemned criminal, a prisoner, a laborer. But in 671 BCE, the substitute was someone who mattered. Damqi was the son of a high temple official in the Babylonian city of Akkad.

The king's agent in Babylonia reported his end. Damqi, "who had ruled Assyria, Babylonia and all the countries, died with his queen," as a substitute for the king. "He and his queen were decorated, treated, displayed, buried and wailed over."

Then the report adds something else. Before the ritual, a prophetess had told Damqi: "You will take over the kingship!"

She was right.

Whether that prophecy is why he was chosen, the letter does not say. It says only that the rituals "succeeded well indeed," and that "the king, my lord, can be glad."

The scholars carried on, as they wrote, "as our fathers did for their lords." In the summer of 669 BCE, they set the day for another substitute to go to his fate. That autumn, on the march to Egypt, Esarhaddon fell ill, and died.

The ritual was far older than him. Its earliest story comes from a Babylonian chronicle, and it is set more than a thousand years before Esarhaddon, in the city of Isin.

There, the chronicle says, King Erra-imitti made his gardener, Enlil-bani, substitute king, and placed the royal tiara on his head. Then Erra-imitti died in his own palace, sipping a hot soup.

The gardener, sitting on the throne, did not give it up.

Enlil-bani ruled Isin for twenty-four years, and his own inscriptions survive. He rebuilt the city wall and named it "Enlil-bani is firm as to foundation." He cut the barley tax from a fifth to a tenth.

Historians suspect the tale is a legend, perhaps one that dressed up an ordinary seizure of the throne.

But in every letter from Nineveh, the ritual ends the same way: the stand-in dies, and the king lives, for a while. Only the oldest story ends the other way. The gods took the king. And the man crowned to die kept the crown.
