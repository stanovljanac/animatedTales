DESCRIPTION:
When the moon went dark over ancient Assyria, the king's scholars had a way to save his life. They put another man on his throne, and when the danger had passed, they let that man die in the king's place.

In the seventh century BCE, Assyria was ruled by Esarhaddon, and hundreds of letters from his scholars survive to show how the substitute king ritual was done. A stand-in was washed, anointed and dressed in the king's robes, with a young woman beside him as his queen. The omens were recited before the sun god Shamash, and the real king stayed hidden in his palace while his scholars wrote to him as "the farmer".

In 671 BCE the substitute was someone who mattered: Damqi, the son of a high temple official in the Babylonian city of Akkad, who had been told by a prophetess that he would take over the kingship. The ritual itself was far older than Esarhaddon. Its earliest story comes from a Babylonian chronicle set more than a thousand years earlier in the city of Isin, and it ends the other way round, though historians suspect that tale is a legend.
