# The Substitute King of Assyria: How Esarhaddon's Scholars Moved Death Onto Another Man

[![The substitute king ritual of ancient Assyria, as told in the 2D animated story of Esarhaddon]({{THUMBNAIL}})]({{VIDEO_URL}})

Some of the letters Assyrian scholars sent to their king open with a title that was not his. "To the farmer, my lord," they begin, and the man they are addressing is Esarhaddon, king of Assyria and of Babylonia. While the scribe wrote, a different man was sitting on the royal throne in the royal robes, and that man had been chosen to die.

This was the substitute king ritual, and we can follow it step by step, because the men who ran it reported on it in writing, to the king they were protecting.

## The sky as writing

Mesopotamian scholars called the stars the writing of heaven, and reading it was a profession. The great reference work for the job was an omen series known as Enūma Anu Enlil, and the gravest sign in it for a king was an eclipse. The moon was read like a map. Its disc was divided into four quarters standing for four lands: Akkad in the south, Elam in the east, Amurru in the west and Subartu in the north. A shadow on one quarter threatened that land. For an Assyrian king the quarter that mattered was Subartu, and Esarhaddon, who also ruled Babylonia, had Akkad to worry about as well, which doubled his exposure.

Yet an omen was a warning rather than a verdict. The scholars had a whole toolkit for turning one aside, including rites called *namburbi* and a ritual known as Bīt rimki. The substitute king was the strongest form of that protection, and at least one letter lists these remedies side by side.

## The paperwork of a borrowed throne

The letters were found in the Kuyunjik archives at Nineveh, the Assyrian capital. Parpola edited them in 1993 as volume ten of the State Archives of Assyria, usually abbreviated SAA 10, and the online edition at ORACC lets anyone read the translations. Most are dated to about 679 to 669 BCE. The writers include the chief scribe and the chief exorcist, and in Parpola's reconstruction it was the exorcist who went looking for the stand-in: a condemned criminal, a prisoner of war, a political adversary or a menial laborer, someone whose death would not stir up trouble.

Then came the enthronement. One scholar reports that the substitute king and his queen were "treated with wine, washed with water and anointed with oil", and that birds were cooked and fed to them. The omens, written down, were recited in front of the sun god Shamash. Another scholar describes hearing them recited a second time and then bending down to bind the written signs into the hem of the substitute's garment, as if to sew the danger shut. One letter, SAA 10 2, even records the substitute speaking: he cried out, "Because of what ominous sign have you enthroned a substitute king?", and then, the report says, denounced a conspiracy against the king.

Meanwhile the real king stayed away from view, and one letter says that during the dangerous period "he may not go into open country". The reign of the stand-in was timed. "He should sit for 100 days," the king had instructed, and in reply his agent in Babylonia, Mar-Issar, wrote that the substitute "has completed the 100 days". Shorter reigns are recorded too, of 47, 20, 7 and even 3 days. When the time was up the letters use the same phrase every time: the substitute was to "go to his fate". The death itself is never described. One modern suggestion is an overdose of a sedative, but the sources do not say.

## Damqi of Akkad

In 671 BCE the stand-in was not a nobody. Damqi was the son of the prelate of Akkad, the temple administrator of that Babylonian city. Mar-Issar reported his end in a letter that is damaged in places, with the missing words restored in brackets by its editor. Damqi died with his queen, and the report lists what was done for the pair: they were decorated, treated, displayed, buried and wailed over. In other words, the stand-in received the funeral of a king.

Then the letter adds something odd. Mar-Issar had heard that a prophetess, before the ceremonies, had told Damqi that he would take over the kingship. Why Damqi was chosen is not stated, and the prophecy may or may not have been the reason. Parpola's own list of likely candidates includes political adversaries, so the question stays open.

The same letter also looks ahead. It recommends that "a common man should, as before, be appointed to the office of the prelate", so that he "may serve as a substitute" at the next eclipse to afflict Babylonia. The words "as before" are all we are given about how often this had happened.

## Did it work?

By the standards of the letters it did. The rituals, Mar-Issar wrote, "succeeded well indeed", and the king "can be glad". The scholars kept going. A letter of 669 BCE says that the "farmer" had already done this "once and twice", and that they should do the same again, "as our fathers did for their lords". It asks for the next stand-in to "go to his fate" so that the writer can perform his ritual "on the 16th day as before". Modern counts of how many times Esarhaddon used the ritual differ, from four in one account to seven in another.

The Babylonian Chronicle describes what happened next. In the twelfth year of his reign the king marched to Egypt, "became ill on the way and died on the tenth day of the month Arahsamna", in October or November of 669 BCE. The chronicle names no place. Esarhaddon had outlived Damqi by two years.

## The gardener of Isin

The oldest known story of the ritual runs the other way. It appears in a Babylonian text called the Chronicle of Early Kings, written down long after the events it describes, and it is set more than a thousand years before Esarhaddon, in the city of Isin. King Erra-imitti, it says, installed his gardener, Enlil-bani, as substitute king and put the royal tiara on his head. Then the king died in his palace, "when he sipped a hot soup". One modern translator reads the dish as hot porridge taken in little sips. In either version, the gardener who sat on the throne "did not give it up and so he was sovereign".

Enlil-bani is a real king of Isin, and he left inscriptions. A king list gives him twenty-four years. By his own account he rebuilt a dilapidated wall of Isin and named it "Enlil-bani is firm as to foundation", and he says he reduced the barley tax from a fifth to a tenth. Historians still suspect the chronicle story is a legend, perhaps a polite costume for an ordinary seizure of power.

## An echo in Babylon

Many scholars also read a famous episode in Babylon in 323 BCE as the same ritual. Arrian, citing Aristobulus, and Plutarch both tell of a stranger found sitting silent on Alexander the Great's empty throne, wearing the diadem and the robe, who was put to death on the advice of the seers. Alexander died on 11 June. That the story is a substitute king ritual is a modern reading, and neither ancient writer calls it one.

The shape of the story never really changes. In every letter from Nineveh the stand-in dies and the king is told he can be glad. Only the oldest tale lets the borrowed crown stay where it was put. Between those two endings sits the question the ritual could never settle: whom does a borrowed crown really protect?
