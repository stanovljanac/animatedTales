# Research — The Substitute King (stage 03)

TOPIC
New idea #32 in `docs/ideas.md` (added 2026-10-27, outside the original thirty): the Mesopotamian
substitute-king ritual (*šar pūḫi*). When an eclipse foretold the king's death, another man was put
on the throne to take the omen, and was then put to death. The story follows the case best
documented in Esarhaddon's letters, and ends with the oldest story of the ritual, in which the king
died and the substitute kept the throne.

RECOMMENDED STORY
In seventh-century Assyria, scholars read eclipses as warnings that the king would die. They also
believed a warning could be redirected. So when the shadow fell on the king's lands, the king stepped
down and hid in his palace as "the farmer". A substitute was washed, anointed, dressed in the royal
robes, given a queen, and made to take the written omens on himself in front of the sun god. He
could sit on the throne for up to a hundred days, and then he "went to his fate". In 671 BCE the
substitute was not a nobody: Damqî, son of the temple official of Akkad, died with his queen, and was
buried and mourned as a king. The same report adds that before the ritual a prophetess had told him,
"You will take over the kingship!" The ritual went on, "as our fathers did for their lords". In the
summer of 669 the scholars set the day for yet another substitute to go to his fate. That autumn
Esarhaddon fell ill on the road to Egypt and died. The ritual was far older than him. Its earliest
story, from a Babylonian chronicle, is set more than a thousand years earlier in Isin. King
Erra-imitti put his gardener on the throne as substitute and died in his palace sipping hot soup. The
gardener did not give the throne up and ruled for twenty-four years.

STORY ANGLE
A ritual that works by moving death from one man to another, told through the scholars' own
letters. They are written to a king who is hiding under the name "the farmer", and they report a
stand-in's death as good news ("the king, my lord, can be glad"). The angle is not "ancient people
were superstitious". It is that the system ran on paperwork and was recorded by the people who ran it.
The turn is that it never really saved anyone: the king it protected died two years after Damqî, and
in the oldest story the gods took the king and left the stand-in on the throne.

ONE-SENTENCE HOOK (verbatim into ideas.md and the script)
When the moon went dark over ancient Assyria, the king's scholars had a way to save his life. They put
another man on his throne — and when the danger had passed, they let that man die in the king's
place.

HISTORICAL PERIOD
Esarhaddon, king of Assyria, 681–669 BCE. The substitute-king letters in SAA 10 are dated by the
editor (Parpola) mostly to 679–669 BCE. Damqî's burial report (SAA 10 352) is dated 671 BCE.
Esarhaddon died on 10 Arahsamna (October/November) 669 BCE. Erra-imitti of Isin reigned
c. 1868–1861 BCE and Enlil-bani c. 1860–1837 BCE (middle chronology). The chronicle telling their
story was written down much later.

LOCATION / CULTURAL CONTEXT
Nineveh (the Assyrian capital, where the letters were found, in the Kuyunjik archives) and the city
of Akkad in Babylonia, where substitutes for the Babylonian half of the realm were enthroned (SAA 10
351, 352). Esarhaddon ruled both Assyria and Babylonia, so an eclipse "afflicting" either land
could call for a substitute. Isin was a city-state in southern Mesopotamia in the early second
millennium BCE.

STORY TYPE
Ritual / historical practice, told as a civilization story: belief → mechanism → one documented
victim → whether it worked → the oldest story, with the opposite ending.

KEY CHARACTERS
- **Esarhaddon**: king of Assyria and Babylonia. Hundreds of letters and reports from his scholars
  survive, and the substitute-king letters are addressed to him, often as "the farmer".
- **The scholars**: Issar-šumu-ereš (chief scribe), Nabû-zeru-lešir, Adad-šumu-uṣur (chief
  exorcist), Akkullanu. They are not named in the script; they speak only through quotations, as
  "one scholar" and "another".
- **Mar-Issar**: the king's agent in Babylonia. He wrote the report of Damqî's death. In the script
  he is "the king's agent in Babylonia".
- **Damqî**: son of the *šatammu* (temple administrator, "prelate") of Akkad. Substitute king who
  died with his queen in 671 BCE.
- **The prophetess**: unnamed in the source. She told Damqî he would take over the kingship.
- **Erra-imitti**: king of Isin, the first king known to have used the ritual, according to a late
  chronicle.
- **Enlil-bani**: his gardener, the substitute who kept the throne. A real king of Isin with his own
  inscriptions.

CENTRAL STORY QUESTION
Can a man really die in someone else's place, and who does the ritual end up saving?

CORE CONFLICT
The king against the sky. The price is paid by whoever sits on the throne in his stead, and the
scholars who run the ritual have to keep it working.

TURNING POINT
Damqî's report. Until then the ritual is a mechanism. The report gives it a name, a queen, a
burial, and a prophecy that came true in the cruellest way. Right after it, the king the ritual
protected dies on the road to Egypt.

PAYOFF
In every letter from Nineveh the ritual ends the same way: the stand-in dies, and the king lives,
for a while. Only the oldest story ends the other way. The gods took the king, and the man crowned
to die kept the crown, ruled for twenty-four years, rebuilt his city's wall and cut the barley tax
in half.

KEY EVENTS

Primary texts: S. Parpola, *Letters from Assyrian and Babylonian Scholars* (SAA 10, 1993), quoted
from the online edition at ORACC (http://oracc.org/saao/), checked in this session. The ABC 20
chronicle is quoted from Livius (adapted from Grayson 1975 and Glassner 2004). Category in brackets:
HR = historical record, TR = tradition/belief, ID = interpretation/debate.

1. **The sky as writing.** Mesopotamian scholars read celestial signs as messages from the gods. The
   standard omen series is *Enūma Anu Enlil*; the stars were called "the writing of heaven". An eclipse
   was the gravest sign for a king. The moon's disc was split into four quarters standing for four
   lands: Akkad (south), Elam (east), Amurru (west) and Subartu (north). A darkened quarter meant
   danger for that land; for an Assyrian king, Subartu, and for Esarhaddon also Akkad (Wikipedia,
   Substitute king ritual, citing Parpola 1983). [TR as belief; HR that they practised it]
2. **A warning, not a verdict.** Omens could be averted by ritual: apotropaic rites (*namburbi*),
   *Bīt rimki*, *Bīt salā' mê*. The substitute king was the strongest form of this (SAA 10 352 lists
   them together). [HR]
3. **Choosing the substitute.** The chief exorcist looked for "a condemned criminal, a prisoner of
   war, a political adversary, or a menial laborer", someone whose death "would not cause a great
   deal of unrest" (Wikipedia, citing Parpola 1983). [ID, Parpola's synthesis]
4. **The enthronement.** SAA 10 2 (Nabû-zeru-lešir, 679 or 674 BCE): "I wrote down whatever signs
   there were, be they celestial, terrestrial or of malformed births, and had them recited in front of
   Šamaš, one after the other. They (the substitute king and queen) were treated with wine, washed with
   water and anointed with oil; I had those birds cooked and made them eat them. The substitute king of
   the land of Akkad took the signs on himself." SAA 10 189 (Adad-šumu-uṣur): "the clothes of the
   king, my lord, … the necklace [of go]ld, the sceptre and the throne". [HR]
5. **Binding the omens.** SAA 10 12 (Issar-šumu-ereš, 671 BCE): "after we had enthroned him, we had
   him hear them in front of Šamaš. Furthermore, yesterday I had him hear them again, and I bent down
   and bound them in his hem." The written record was tied to the substitute's clothes to make the
   transfer permanent (Wikipedia, citing Parpola). [HR]
6. **The farmer.** The real king stayed in seclusion and did not travel. His scholars addressed him
   as "the farmer". SAA 10 2: "To the 'farmer,' my lord"; SAA 10 1: "[To] the 'farmer,' [our] lord".
   SAA 10 352: during the eclipse period "he may not go into open country". [HR]
7. **A hundred days.** SAA 10 350 (Mar-Issar, 671 BCE): "Con[cerning the su]bstitute king about whom
   [the king, my lord, wrote to me]: 'He should sit for 100 days' — [now then] he has completed the
   100 days". Shorter reigns are recorded too (47, 20, 7, 3 days; Wikipedia, citing Parpola). [HR]
8. **"Go to his fate".** SAA 10 220 and 221 (669 BCE): "let him complete the 100 days"; "Let the
   substitute [kin]g [g]o to his fate and let me perform my ritual on the 16th day as before". The
   death itself is never described; the method is unknown (Wikipedia mentions a possible overdose of a
   "soporific"). [HR for the phrase; ID for the method]
9. **Damqî (671 BCE).** SAA 10 352 (Mar-Issar): "[Damqî], the son of the prelate of Akka[d], who had
   ru[led] Assyria, Babylon(ia) [and] all the countries, [di]ed with his queen on the night o[f the xth
   day as] a substitute for the king, my lord, [and for the sake of the li]fe of Šamaš-šumu-uki[n]. He
   went to his fate for their redemption. We prepared the burial chamber. He and his queen were
   decorated, treated, displayed, buried and wailed over." [HR]
10. **The prophetess.** Same letter: "[I] have heard that before these ceremonies a prophetess had
    prophesied, saying to the son of the prelate, Damqî: 'You will take over the kingship!' … These
    apotropaic rituals which were performed succeeded well indeed; the king, my lord, can be glad."
    [HR] Whether the prophecy is why Damqî was chosen is not stated. Parpola's list of candidates
    includes "a political adversary", so the reading is open. [ID: the script says the letter does
    not say]
11. **"As our fathers did".** SAA 10 221 (669 BCE): "As our fathers did for their lords, and (as) the
    'farmer' has already done once and twice … just so let us also do now." [HR]
12. **Esarhaddon's death.** ABC 14 / Babylonian Chronicle: in his twelfth year the king of Assyria
    marched to Egypt, "became ill on the way and died on the tenth day of the month Arahsamna"
    (669 BCE). The chronicle names no place. [HR]
13. **Isin.** ABC 20, Chronicle of Early Kings, A 31–36: "Irra-imitti, the king, installed Enlil-bani,
    the gardener, as substitute king on his throne. He placed the royal tiara on his head. Irra-imitti
    died in his palace when he sipped a hot soup. Enlil-bani, who occupied the throne, did not give it
    up and so he was sovereign." Glassner translates the dish as "hot porridge in little sips"
    (*pappasu*); Grayson and Livius give "soup". [TR, late chronicle; the earliest mention of the
    ritual]
14. **Enlil-bani the king.** Twenty-four years according to the Ur-Isin king list. His own
    inscriptions say he rebuilt "the wall of Isin which had become dilapidated" and named it
    *Enlil-bāni-išdam-kīn*, "Enlil-bāni is firm as to foundation". CBS 13909: "In Nippur I established
    justice, and promoted righteousness … I reduced the barley-tax, which had been at one-fifth, to
    one-tenth" (Wikipedia, Enlil-bani). [HR]

CAUSES
- Belief that eclipses announced the death of a land's king, and that an omen could be averted by
  ritual.
- A king who ruled two lands, so he was exposed to two sets of eclipses, and who leaned heavily on
  his scholars.

CONSEQUENCES
- Real people were put to death as stand-ins. One of them, Damqî, is known by name, with his burial
  recorded.
- The real king spent long stretches in seclusion. If Esarhaddon did it seven times at up to a
  hundred days each, that is close to a fifth of those years (Wikipedia, citing Jones 2021). [ID —
  not used]
- In the oldest story, a gardener became king.

MOST INTERESTING FACTS
- The king's own scholars addressed him as "the farmer" while the substitute reigned.
- The omens were read to the substitute in front of the sun god and tied into the hem of his robe.
- Damqî's burial list: "decorated, treated, displayed, buried and wailed over".
- A prophetess told Damqî he would take over the kingship, and he did.
- The report on his death ends: "the king, my lord, can be glad."
- Esarhaddon died two years later on the road to Egypt.
- The gardener-king named his city wall after his own firmness and halved the barley tax.

MYTHS / LEGENDS / TRADITIONS
- The power of omens and of the ritual is Mesopotamian belief. The script frames it through what
  the scholars believed and did; it never presents the transfer as working.
- The Isin story is from a late chronicle and is generally treated as legendary, perhaps apocryphal.
  It may dress up a usurpation (Wikipedia, Enlil-bani / Erra-imitti). The script says once:
  "historians suspect the tale is a legend".

UNCERTAINTIES / DEBATES
- **How many times under Esarhaddon.** Wikipedia gives seven (citing Jones 2021); the Bīt rimki
  article says four. The script avoids a number and quotes "once and twice" from SAA 10 221.
- **Damqî's date.** Dated 671 BCE in SAA 10 (the editor's dating). The script uses 671.
- **Why Damqî.** Not stated. He was unusual: Mar-Issar's same letter recommends that "a common man
  should, as before, be appointed to the office of the prelate" so that he "may serve as a substitute"
  at the next eclipse afflicting Babylonia. Not used in the script; too intricate for the time.
- **Between Erra-imitti and Enlil-bani**, two variant chronicle copies list an Ikūn-pî-Ištar for six
  months or a year. Not used.
- **Soup or porridge.** *Pappasu*. The script uses "hot soup" (Grayson / Livius).
- **The substitute's power.** Some letters suggest he had a court and some role (see the UT Austin
  piece). Not used.

ESSENTIAL MATERIAL
- Hook: another man on the throne, who dies in the king's place.
- Eclipses as "the writing of heaven"; quarters of the moon as lands; a warning that can be
  redirected.
- The enthronement: washed, anointed, robes, a queen; the signs recited before Shamash; "took the
  signs on himself"; the hem.
- The farmer; up to a hundred days; "went to his fate".
- Damqî: who he was, his death with his queen, the burial list.
- The prophetess: "You will take over the kingship!"; "the king, my lord, can be glad."
- 669: another substitute; Esarhaddon dies on the road to Egypt.
- Isin: the gardener, the tiara, the hot soup, the throne kept, twenty-four years.

SUPPORTING (kept)
- "As our fathers did for their lords" — the ritual as inherited routine.
- Enlil-bani's wall name and the barley tax — proof the gardener-king was real.
- "Historians suspect the tale is a legend."

OPTIONAL (cut for time)
- The substitute who cried out, "Because of what ominous sign have you enthroned a substitute king?"
  and then denounced a conspiracy against the king (SAA 10 2). Strong material, but it splits the
  story's focus.
- Akkullanu's scolding letter to Ashurbanipal (SAA 10 90): "Why you? And why an evil of
  Babylonia?", and Ashurbanipal using a statue as substitute instead of a man.
- Alexander the Great at Babylon, May 323 BCE. A stranger was found silent on the empty throne
  wearing the diadem and robe and was put to death on the seers' advice (Arrian 7.24, from
  Aristobulus; Plutarch, *Alexander* 73–74). Alexander died on 11 June. Many scholars read it as the
  substitute ritual. [ID] Excellent material for a sequel or a Short.
- Mar-Issar's advice to appoint "a common man" as prelate.
- The Hebrew-Bible link (Esarhaddon as the son of Sennacherib, murdered by his sons in 681).
- The later echoes: Herodotus 7 (Xerxes and Artabanus), Shah Abbas in 1591.

STORY STRUCTURE
Beginning: the hook (another man dies in the king's place), then the sky as writing and the omen as
a redirectable warning.
Development: Esarhaddon and his scholars; the enthronement, the omens before Shamash, the hem; the
farmer; a hundred days; "went to his fate".
Escalation: Damqî, with a name, a queen and a royal burial; the prophetess; "the king can be glad".
Turning point: the ritual continues "as our fathers did", and Esarhaddon dies anyway, on the road
to Egypt.
Payoff: the oldest story. Isin, the gardener, the tiara, the hot soup, the throne kept for
twenty-four years. Every letter ends with the stand-in dead; only the oldest story ends with the
king dead and the substitute crowned.

RECOMMENDED VOICE
Ancient / mysterious.

VOICE CHARACTER
Deep, deliberate, slightly dark. He sounds like someone reading old letters by lamplight who finds
them stranger the longer he reads.

VOICE DELIVERY
~145 wpm, not slower. Keep the sky section atmospheric and unhurried. Read the scholars' quotations
plainly and correctly, like routine paperwork; that flatness is what makes them chilling. Pause
before "To the farmer, my lord." Read the burial list as a list, with even weight on every verb. A
short pause after the prophetess's line, then say "She was right" quietly. Esarhaddon's death is
flat, not tragic. Lighten slightly for the gardener: the soup is dry irony, not a joke. Land the last
two sentences slowly.

WHY THIS VOICE FITS
The subject is ancient religion: omens, the sky, a death moved from one man to another. The
material is uncanny rather than violent; nobody is shown dying. An atmospheric, deliberate voice
lets the strangeness of the letters do the work. A dramatic voice would turn the ritual into horror.
