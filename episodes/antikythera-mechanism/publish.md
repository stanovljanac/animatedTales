DESCRIPTION:
In 1900, a boat of Greek sponge divers sheltering from a storm found an ancient shipwreck off the island of Antikythera. Among the statues they raised was a shapeless lump of corroded bronze that nobody paid much attention to.

Two years later the lump split apart, and archaeologist Valerios Stais saw something that did not seem to belong in the ancient world: a gear wheel. Then another, and another, packed inside a case the size of a shoebox. For decades the Antikythera mechanism stayed a puzzle, until X-ray scans in 2005 revealed about thirty surviving gears and thousands of tiny letters hidden beneath the corrosion.

Turn its handle and the machine moved the sky. It tracked the Sun and the Moon through the zodiac, predicted eclipses years in advance, kept track of the Olympic Games, and even copied the uneven speed of the real Moon. So how was something this intricate built in ancient Greece, and why is it the only one we have?
