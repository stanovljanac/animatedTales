Divers pulled this machine from a shipwreck. Then scientists realized what it could calculate.

In the spring of 1900, a boat of Greek sponge divers was running from a storm. They sheltered off Antikythera, a small rocky island between Crete and mainland Greece. When the sea calmed, one diver went down to look around.

According to the story the crew told, he came back up terrified. He said the seabed was covered in bodies. Men and horses, lying in the dark.

His captain went down to see for himself. The bodies were statues. Bronze and marble, the cargo of a ship that had sunk more than two thousand years earlier, probably on its way to Italy.

With help from the Greek navy, the divers spent months bringing it up. In canvas suits and copper helmets, at depths their bodies were never built for, the work was brutal. One diver died. Two were left paralyzed.

They raised statues, glass and pottery. They also raised a shapeless lump of corroded bronze and rotting wood. Nobody paid it much attention.

Then, in 1902, the lump split apart. An archaeologist named Valerios Stais looked closer, and saw something that did not seem to belong in the ancient world. A gear wheel.

And not just one. Layers of gears, packed inside a case about the size of a shoebox. Some experts insisted it had to be a later object that had fallen onto the wreck. Nothing this intricate could be that old.

For decades it stayed a puzzle. Then, in 2005, researchers scanned every fragment with X-rays in extraordinary detail. They found about thirty surviving gears, and thousands of tiny letters hidden beneath the corrosion.

Slowly, its purpose became clear.

You turned a handle on the side. And as the gears moved, the machine moved the sky.

On the front, pointers carried the Sun and the Moon through the zodiac, around a calendar of three hundred and sixty-five days. A small turning ball, half dark and half bright, showed the phase of the Moon.

On the back, spiral dials counted long cycles of time. One predicted eclipses, years in advance. Another kept track of the great athletic games of Greece, including the Olympics.

The most astonishing detail was the Moon. It does not cross the sky at a steady speed, and Greek astronomers knew it. So the builder set two gears on slightly offset axles, joined by a pin sliding in a slot. The Moon's pointer sped up and slowed down, just like the real Moon.

It was probably built in the second century BCE, though the exact date is still debated. Nothing of comparable complexity is known again for more than a thousand years.

So is this a machine that shouldn't exist?

Not quite. The Roman writer Cicero described devices that showed the motions of the Sun, the Moon and the planets. One, he wrote, was built by Archimedes. For centuries, that sounded like exaggeration.

The Antikythera mechanism proves it was not.

Which leaves a harder question. If the Greeks could build one, they almost certainly built others. But bronze was valuable, and old metal was melted down and made into something new.

This one survived only because it never reached the shore. The same sea that swallowed the ship kept the machine safe for two thousand years.
