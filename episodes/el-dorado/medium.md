# El Dorado: The Gilded Man of Lake Guatavita and the Chronicler Who Went Looking for His Gold

[![El Dorado, the gilded man on a reed raft in Lake Guatavita, in the 2D animated story of the legend]({{THUMBNAIL}})]({{VIDEO_URL}})

Before El Dorado was a city, a kingdom or a lake on European maps, it was the title of one person at one moment: an heir, stripped, painted with sticky earth and dusted with powdered gold, standing on a reed raft in the middle of a high mountain lake in what is now Colombia. The Spaniards spent two centuries chasing the name. The ceremony it came from they never saw.

## A chronicler and his informant

The fullest account comes from Juan Rodríguez Freyle, a chronicler born in Bogotá, who set it down in *El Carnero*, written between 1636 and 1638. He says he was writing on his seventieth birthday, 25 April 1636, and that he had the story from a friend: don Juan, cacique of Guatavita. Don Juan was the nephew of the ruler the conquistadors found on the throne, and when the Spaniards entered the highlands he was himself in the fast that prepared an heir for office.

That chain matters, and it is worth holding in mind for the rest of the story. Freyle wrote about a century after the conquest, and from one informant.

## Six years in the dark

According to Freyle, an heir of Guatavita prepared in a cave for six years. He was kept from women and from meat, salt and chili, and from the sun: he came out only at night, to look at the moon and the stars. When the fast ended, the first place he was taken was the lake.

## The raft on the lake

Freyle's description of the ceremony is precise. The Muisca built a great raft of reeds, as showy as they could make it, and set four braziers on it that burned incense, turpentine and other perfumes. Crowds in feathers and gold crowns stood all around the lake, with fires along the shore, and the smoke was thick enough to block the daylight.

On the raft the heir was stripped, smeared with a sticky earth and dusted with gold until he was covered. A heap of gold and emeralds lay at his feet as an offering to his god, and four principal chiefs stood with him, also naked, each with an offering of his own. Cornets and conch trumpets sounded and the shore shouted until the raft reached the middle of the lake. There a flag was raised and the noise stopped. The heir threw his gold into the water, the chiefs threw theirs, the flag came down, and the shouting and the dancing began again. The new lord was recognised.

It was a gift, not a hoard. Freyle adds that the offerings were not only for coronations: a yearly festival, *correr la tierra*, ended with three days of drinking and burning incense at Guatavita, while rafts carried the gold the people had to the middle of the lake. The Muisca were a set of chiefdoms around Guatavita, Bogotá, Tunja and Sogamoso rather than an empire, and the region had few gold deposits of its own, so most of its gold came through trade. ## From a man to a map

Freyle says the name began in Quito. There, Sebastián de Belalcázar met an Indian of the Bogotá country who told him that, to make a king, the people took him to a great lake and gilded him. "Let us go and look for this gilded Indian," Belalcázar answered. The chronicler Oviedo wrote down a similar Quito story in 1541, about a lord who went about continually covered in gold dust as fine as ground salt.

Three captains reached the highlands: Quesada in March 1537, with the 173 men (some sources say 180) who reached Muisca land out of about 800, and Federmann and Belalcázar at the end of 1538. What they found was gold and emeralds, but no golden city. The name then left the man. In July 1539 a witness described an expedition that had set out from Popayán in search of "a land called el dorado", which historians cite as the first time the phrase appears as a place. After that it kept growing: a city, a kingdom with names like Manoa and Omagua, and finally a lake called Parime, drawn on European maps from 1598 into the early nineteenth century.

Walter Raleigh sailed after it in 1595 and again in 1617. On the second voyage his son Walter was killed and Captain Kemys took his own life, and in 1618 King James had Raleigh executed. Freyle, who headed his chapter on the subject "this deceptive name", was blunt about the cost: it was the name that cost so many lives and fortunes.

## The lake that kept its gold

The lake itself resisted. Freyle writes as an eyewitness here: Antonio de Sepúlveda agreed with King Philip II to drain Guatavita, and that the first cut was still visible in his day. Sepúlveda claimed that more than twelve thousand pesos had been taken from the exposed edges alone. Later he wanted to drain it deeper and could not. He died poor and tired, Freyle says, and adds that he knew him well and helped bury him in the church of Guatavita. Others tried and gave up, because the lagoon is very deep, full of mud, and needs a great deal of money and many people. Even the year of Sepúlveda's licence is disputed, 1562 in one source and the 1580s in another, a reminder of how thin the record is beyond Freyle's pages.

## The raft from the cave

In 1969 a farmer, Cruz María Dimaté, described gold and pottery from a cave on the hill La Campana, near Pasca, to the parish priest, Jaime Hincapié Santamaría. Among it was a gold raft in the care of the Museo del Oro, 19.5 centimeters long, with eleven figures. A large chief sits in the middle on a stool, arms folded across his chest, wearing a headdress, ear pendants and a rectangular nose ornament. Around him stand ten smaller figures: two large masked ones with maracas, two seated with poporos, and six standing. None of them faces the chief. The whole raft was cast in a single pour by the lost-wax method, in an alloy of about sixty-five percent gold with silver and copper, and charcoal from the mould dates it to about 1295 to 1410, before Columbus.

A second raft, from the Siecha lagoon, was found in 1856 and was reportedly destroyed in a fire at the port of Bremen on its way to a Berlin museum.

## What nobody saw

Does the Pasca raft show the Guatavita ceremony? The Museo del Oro and Spink read it that way. Other anthropologists and historians say it need not: lake ceremonies are documented across Muisca territory, and the raft was found at Pasca, not by Guatavita.

The ceremony itself is harder still, because nobody who wrote it down saw it. The Museo del Oro's own text says the conquerors never saw it, and PBS notes the ritual was no longer practiced by the time they arrived. One scholar, Quintero-Guzmán, suggests the Guatavita ceremony may have been a one-time event. Historians disagree about how far to trust the reports: Bray holds that the Spaniards heard them from Muisca witnesses, while Ramos Pérez and Hemming argue that the Spaniards invented the story.

## The chronicler goes hunting

The sharpest moment in the whole story is Freyle's own confession. In the chapter on the five sacred lakes, Guatavita, Guasca, Siecha, Teusacá and Ubaque, he admits that he went after the gold himself. Teusacá was said to hold two golden caimans. "I confess my sin," he writes, in Spanish, "I entered this litany with greed to fish out one of the caimans." He courted a priest who had once served that shrine until the man agreed to lead him there.

"As soon as we uncovered the lake and he saw the water, he fell face down on the ground, and I could never raise him, nor get another word out of him. I left him there and came back with nothing, and with the loss of what I had spent." Freyle never says why the priest fell. He adds a remark that could be a motto for the whole legend: gold and silver are a great sweet, and children and old men run after it and are never full.
