El Dorado was not a city. It was a man — naked, covered in gold dust, standing on a raft in the middle of a mountain lake.

The best-known account was written in 1636 by Juan Rodríguez Freyle, a chronicler from Bogotá. He had it from a friend: don Juan, the Muisca lord of Guatavita, whose uncle ruled when the Spaniards arrived. Don Juan was in the middle of an heir's fast at that moment.

That was the custom, Freyle writes. An heir spent six years in a cave. No women, no meat, no salt, no chili. He came out only at night, to look at the moon and the stars.

Then came the lake. On Lake Guatavita the people built a great raft of reeds and set four braziers on it, burning incense until the smoke dimmed the daylight. Crowds ringed the shore in feathers and gold crowns. They stripped the heir, smeared him with sticky earth, and dusted him with powdered gold until he was covered from head to foot.

He stood on the raft with a heap of gold and emeralds at his feet and four chiefs beside him. Horns sounded and the shore roared until they reached the middle of the lake. Then a flag went up for silence, and he threw the gold into the water. The chiefs threw theirs. When the flag came down, the noise began again. He was lord.

It was not treasure being hoarded. It was a gift. The Muisca got most of their gold through trade, and what they made of it they gave to their gods, in lakes and rivers.

The Spaniards heard of it in Quito, from a man of the Bogotá country. "Let us go and find this gilded Indian," said the conquistador Sebastián de Belalcázar. Three Spanish captains reached the highlands. They found gold and emeralds. They did not find a golden city.

The name soon left the man. It became a place, then a city, then a lake called Parime, drawn on European maps for two hundred years. Walter Raleigh sailed after it twice; the second time his son died, and the king had Raleigh executed. Freyle called it a name that "has cost so many lives and fortunes."

The lake kept most of its secrets. Antonio de Sepúlveda won a licence from Philip the Second to drain it, cut a channel, and said he took more than twelve thousand pesos from the exposed shore. Years later he tried to drain it deeper and could not. He died poor, and Freyle, who knew him, helped bury him.

A clue turned up in a cave. In 1969 a farmer at Pasca found gold and pottery and told his priest. Among it was a gold raft twenty centimeters long: a seated chief, ten smaller figures around him, some masked, all cast in one pour. Charcoal from its mold dates it to about 1300 to 1400, before Columbus.

Whether it shows Guatavita, scholars argue; it was found at Pasca, not by that lake. And whether the ceremony happened as Freyle tells it, nobody can prove. He wrote it a century after the conquest, and the Spaniards who came hunting never saw it.

Freyle went hunting himself, and he confesses it. He wanted one of two golden caimans said to lie in another sacred lake, Teusacá. He flattered a priest who had once kept that shrine, and the priest led him there. When the water came into view, the priest fell face down on the ground. Freyle could not raise him, and never got another word out of him. "I left him there," he wrote, "and went back with nothing." He does not say why the man fell.

The Spaniards were looking for a place where gold lay for the taking. What the Muisca had was a lake where it was given away.
