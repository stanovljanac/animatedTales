# Research — The Rule Nobody Could Keep in the Land of the Dead (stage 03)

TOPIC
New idea #33 in `docs/ideas.md` (added 2026-11-03, outside the original thirty): one story told on
three continents. A person is brought back from the land of the dead, or almost: a rule is given, the
rule is broken at the last step, and the dead stay dead. Three tellings are read in the source: the
Greek Orpheus, the Japanese Izanagi in the *Kojiki*, and the Cherokee "Daughter of the Sun" in
Mooney. The episode opens the channel's possible "Same Story, Different World" playlist.

RECOMMENDED STORY
Orpheus is the setup the viewer already knows: a musician is allowed to lead his dead wife out of
the underworld if he does not look back, and looks back. Then the two tellings the viewer does not
know. In the *Kojiki* (712 CE) the god Izanagi follows his wife Izanami into the Land of Hades; she
says she has eaten the food of that land, will ask its gods, and tells him "Look not at me!". He
waits, breaks a tooth from his comb, lights it, and finds her rotting with eight thunder gods living
in her body. He flees; she sends a hag, thunder gods and an army after him; a thrown headdress
becomes grapes, a thrown comb bamboo shoots, three peaches put the army to flight. At the Even Pass
of Hades he blocks the road with a boulder that a thousand men could not lift, and across it they
trade a threat and an answer: a thousand of his people strangled a day, against fifteen hundred
birth huts built a day. In the Cherokee tale (Mooney, 1900, headed "Origin of death") the Sun hates
the people and her heat kills them; they try to kill her, and a rattlesnake bites her daughter
instead. To bring her back from the Ghost country in the west, seven men go with a box. They find
the dead dancing, strike her down in the ring, shut her in the box, and are told not to open it,
"even a little way", until they are home. On the road she asks to be let out, then for food, water
and air. Afraid she is dying, they lift the lid a little; something flutters out into the thicket,
a redbird; the box is empty. The tale ends by saying what the failure explains.

STORY ANGLE
Not "three myths are similar". The rule is different every time: do not look at her, do not look
back, do not open the box. What is the same is that it is broken at the last step, and why: Izanagi
"could not wait" (Kojiki), Orpheus wanted to see her, the Cherokee men were afraid she was dying.
Nobody breaks it out of carelessness. The Cherokee tale and the Kojiki are explicit that the failure
is the start of something (death that cannot be undone; a thousand deaths a day answered by births),
so Orpheus, retold in that light, is not only a love story but the Greek share of an
origin-of-death story. The viewer then gets the open question of why three peoples on three
continents tell it, with the competing answers and the honest "nobody knows".

ONE-SENTENCE HOOK (verbatim into ideas.md and the script)
A Greek musician, a Japanese god and seven Cherokee men all went into the land of the dead to bring
someone back. Each was given a rule — and each of them broke it.

HISTORICAL PERIOD
Greek: Orpheus is not in Homer or Hesiod; earliest literary trace is a two-word fragment of Ibycus
(6th c. BCE). The fullest tellings are Roman: Virgil, *Georgics* 4 (29 BCE), and Ovid,
*Metamorphoses* 10 (8 CE). Japanese: *Kojiki*, completed 712 CE (the *Nihon Shoki* followed in 720).
Cherokee: recorded by James Mooney, *Myths of the Cherokee*, 19th Annual Report of the Bureau of
American Ethnology (1900). The Cherokee tale is oral tradition of unknown age; 1900 is the date of
the record, not of the story.

LOCATION / CULTURAL CONTEXT
Greece and Rome (Thrace, the entrance to Hades); Japan (Yamato court at Nara, where the Kojiki was
compiled; the story's own geography ends at the "Ifuya Pass in the land of Idzumo"); the Cherokee
homeland in the southern Appalachians (the Ghost country lies in the west, in the Darkening land).

STORY TYPE
Myth, comparative. Three self-contained tellings, one shared plot, one open question.

KEY CHARACTERS
- **Orpheus**: musician whose lyre moves trees, beasts and stones. His wife is Eurydice.
- **Eurydice**: bitten by a snake and dead. Named first in the literature in the *Lament for Bion*
  (1st c. BCE) per Wikipedia's Orpheus article (citing Freeman 1946).
- **Hades and Persephone**: the king and queen of the dead, who set the condition (Ovid).
- **Izanagi**: the Kojiki's "Male-Who-Invites", creator god who made the lands with his sister-wife.
- **Izanami**: the "Female-Who-Invites", dies giving birth to the fire god; after the pass she
  becomes the Great-Deity-of-Hades.
- **The Ugly-Female-of-Hades, the eight thunder deities, the warriors of Hades**: the pursuers.
- **The Sun's daughter**: killed by the rattlesnake; the dead she is brought back from are
  dancing in the Ghost country.
- **The seven men**: chosen by the people, one sourwood rod each, a box. Unnamed.

CENTRAL STORY QUESTION
What is the rule, why do they all break it, and how can the same story be told so far apart?

CORE CONFLICT
Love and grief against the one condition the dead will not waive.

TURNING POINT
After the three tellings: the rule changes each time, the reason for breaking it does not.

PAYOFF
The Cherokee tale says in one sentence what the other two imply: if the men had kept the box
closed, the dead from the Ghost country could be brought back, "but now when they die we can never
bring them back." The script ends there.

KEY EVENTS

Category in brackets: TR = tradition/myth, HR = historical record (of the texts), ID = interpretation/debate.

1. **Orpheus and Eurydice.** Eurydice dies of a snakebite (Virgil: fleeing Aristaeus; Ovid: dancing
   with naiads on her wedding day; both then the same). Orpheus goes to the underworld, moves its
   rulers with his music (Ovid, Metamorphoses 10). Condition: he must walk in front and not look
   back until both are in the upper world (Ovid 10.50–52 as cited in Wikipedia's Orpheus and
   Eurydice). He turns just before the exit (Ovid 10.53–63) and she is lost. [TR]
2. **What the Greek sources do not say.** Plato's *Symposium* 179d: the gods "showed him only a
   phantom of his wife". Euripides and Plato refer to his descent without naming Eurydice.
   Wikipedia does not say in which source the do-not-look-back condition first appears and neither
   does this file. The script therefore tells the condition as part of the story and says nothing
   about how old it is or which author wrote it first. [HR for the sources; the date of the
   condition is unknown]
3. **Izanami dies.** Burnt giving birth to the Deity-of-Fire; Izanagi cuts the child down and mourns
   (Kojiki, Sections VII–VIII). [TR]
4. **Section IX, "The Land of Hades" (Chamberlain, pp. 38–41; read verbatim in this session).**
   "Thine Augustness my lovely younger sister! the lands that I and thou made are not yet finished
   making; so come back!" She answers: "Lamentable indeed that thou earnest not sooner! I have eaten
   of the furnace of Hades [footnote 3: literally 'of the food of Hades']. Nevertheless, as I
   reverence the entry here of Thine Augustness my lovely elder brother, I wish to return. Moreover
   I will discuss it particularly with the Deities of Hades. Look not at me!" Then: "as she tarried
   there very long, he could not wait." He breaks off one end-tooth of the comb in his left bunch,
   "lit one light and went in and looked. Maggots were swarming, and [she was] rotting", and eight
   thunder deities dwelt in her body. [TR]
5. **The flight.** "Thou hast put me to shame." She sends the Ugly-Female-of-Hades (one pursuer in
   the text). His black head-dress thrown down turns to grapes; his comb thrown down turns to
   bamboo-sprouts; each time she stops to eat. Then the eight Thunder-Deities "with a thousand and
   five hundred warriors of Hades". He draws his sword; at the base of the Even Pass of Hades he
   takes three peaches and "smote [his pursuers therewith], so that they all fled back". [TR]
6. **The pass.** Last of all Izanami comes herself. "So he drew a thousand-draught rock [footnote
   20: 'a rock which it would take a thousand men to lift'], and blocked up the Even Pass of
   Hades". Across it: "I will in one day strangle to death a thousand of the folks of thy land."
   "I will in one day set up a thousand and five hundred parturition-houses." Footnote 21: the sense
   of the leave-taking is uncertain; Moribe reads it as a divorce. She becomes the Great-Deity-of-
   Hades. [TR]
   - **Caution on the numbers.** Chamberlain adds "In this manner each day a thousand people would
     surely be born", which does not match fifteen hundred huts. Other translations (Philippi) say
     fifteen hundred. The script gives only the two figures that are stable: a thousand strangled a
     day, fifteen hundred birth huts a day.
7. **Purification.** Izanagi washes off the pollution of the land; his eyes and nose give Amaterasu,
   Tsukuyomi and Susanoo (Section X). Not told in the script. [TR]
8. **The Cherokee, "The Daughter of the Sun", Mooney, pp. 252–254, headed "Origin of death" (read
   verbatim in this session from Gutenberg).** The Sun hates the people, who squint at her, and
   sends sultry rays: hundreds die of fever. Two attempts to kill her fail (the Spreading-adder and
   the Copperhead); the second, the Rattlesnake, "bites the daughter in the doorway and she dies".
   The Sun grieves and the world is dark. Seven men are chosen, each with a sourwood rod, and a box.
   They travel seven days west to the Darkening land, find the ghosts dancing, strike her once each
   as she passes, and at the seventh round she falls out of the ring. [TR]
9. **The box.** Quoted: "they must be very sure not to open the box, even a little way, until they
   were home again." On the way east she revives and asks to be let out, then for food, water and
   air. "They were afraid she was really dying now, so they lifted the lid a little to give her air,
   but as they did so there was a fluttering sound inside and something flew past them into the
   thicket." "They shut down the lid and went on again to the settlements, but when they got there
   and opened the box it was empty." [TR]
10. **What the tale says it explains.** "So we know the Redbird is the daughter of the Sun, and if
    the men had kept the box closed, as the Little Men told them to do, they would have brought her
    home safely, and we could bring back our other friends also from the Ghost country, but now
    when they die we can never bring them back." Not in the script: the ending of the Sun weeping
    and the dancers who made her smile. [TR]
11. **A fourth telling, optional.** Tachi Yokut, California (A. L. Kroeber, *Indian Myths of South
    Central California*, pp. 216–218; read through a web transcription, not the book). A man follows
    his dead wife to the island of the dead. The chief tells him to hide for six days at home and
    then emerge and dance; he waits five, "was in too much of a hurry", and a rattlesnake kills him.
    The rule here is "do not hurry". There is no looking taboo in it. [TR]

CAUSES
Only what the plot needs: the death, the love, the condition.

CONSEQUENCES
In the Kojiki, from that exchange people die every day and children are born every day. In the
Cherokee tale, the dead cannot be brought back. In Orpheus there is no stated consequence in the
sources read; he loses her again.

MOST INTERESTING FACTS
- The rule is different in each: do not look at her (Kojiki), do not look back (Orpheus), do not open
  the box (Cherokee), do not hurry (Tachi Yokut).
- The reason it is broken is different each time and always understandable: he "could not wait"
  (Kojiki, literally), Orpheus turns in his eagerness (Ovid), the men are afraid she is dying.
- Mooney's own heading is "Origin of death". The Kojiki is the origin of the daily thousand.
- Stith Thompson's Motif-Index classifies the tale type as F81.1 (Orpheus) and notes the conditions
  vary: not to look, not to touch, not to open a bag, not to hurry. Seen only as quoted in a search
  result; do not cite the wording in the script.
- A. H. Gayton wrote "The Orpheus Myth in North America" (*Journal of American Folklore* 48, 1935);
  Åke Hultkrantz devoted a whole monograph to it (*The North American Indian Orpheus Tradition*,
  Stockholm 1957, reissued 2022). The existence of those two works is checked; their contents are not.

MYTHS / LEGENDS / TRADITIONS
Everything above is TR. The script tells each as a story inside its tradition ("the Japanese tell
it", "the Cherokee tell it") and does not stop to say so again; the only evidence-level flags in the
narration are the open question at the end and the one-clause doubt about a very old common source.

UNCERTAINTIES / DEBATES
Why the story exists in three places is not settled, and nothing found proves any answer. Three
positions, each with what was and was not checked:
1. **A very old common source.** Witzel's "Laurasian" model argues for shared, very old myth layers
   across Eurasia and the Americas; a search excerpt of Witzel (2001, in a Quest Journal PDF) lists
   "the Orpheus myth, found in Greece, Japan, N. America" as an example. Not read in full.
   Wikipedia's Comparative mythology page reports Bruce Lincoln calling the model's deep-prehistory
   inferences "ill-founded, ill-conceived, unconvincing". **Do not attribute this to d'Huy**: his
   phylogenetic work in the sources found is on the Cosmic Hunt (the Big Dipper), not on Orpheus.
2. **Shamanic journeys and near-death experiences.** The publisher's summary of Hultkrantz says he
   "found that Native American afterlife myths were actually influenced by NDEs and shamanic otherworld
   journeys" and compares them with Greece, Mesopotamia, Asia and the Pacific. This is the
   publisher's blurb, not Hultkrantz's text. M. L. West (*The Orphic Poems*, 1983) argues that early
   Greek religion was influenced by Central Asian shamanism (bibliography entry, not read).
3. **Shared human experience.** Everyone loses someone and everyone asks why the dead do not return.
   Jung's archetypes are the usual name, and the Wikipedia article itself concedes they "cannot be
   proven". Used in the script only as the plain observation, not as Jung.
Not found and therefore not claimed: that the Native American tales are post-contact borrowing from
Europeans, that Orpheus and Izanagi are related, or that anyone has proven any of the three. The
Wikipedia pages for Orpheus and for Izanagi do not link the two myths at all.

STORY STRUCTURE
Myth / religion, with a mystery tail. Hook (three figures, one rule) → Orpheus as the known
setup → Izanagi (the longest, the most visual) → the Cherokee box → the turn (the rules differ, the
reasons do not) → how can this be (three answers, none proven) → Mooney's sentence as the payoff.

RECOMMENDED VOICE
Ancient / mysterious.

VOICE CHARACTER
Deep, deliberate, slightly dark; someone telling three stories by firelight who does not lean on any
of them.

VOICE DELIVERY
~143 wpm (the user's pace; the body runs ~640 words so the finished video stays under 5:00).
Orpheus quick and quiet, a story the listener already carries. Slow down for Izanagi's torch and
let "Maggots swarmed over her body" fall flat and unpushed. The chase is brisk, light on the
grapes and peaches, which are almost a fairy tale; then slow again for the boulder and the exchange,
read as a calm bargain, not a curse. The Cherokee section is plainer and warmer. Give "She asked
for food. For water. For air." an even weight. The turn paragraph is level; the three reasons are
not a list to rush. The last line is Mooney's and is read without any push.

WHY THIS VOICE FITS
All three tellings are myths of the dead, and the strangeness is the point. A documentary voice
would flatten the Kojiki's horror into a plot summary; a dramatic voice would turn the Cherokee box
into tragedy it does not claim to be.
