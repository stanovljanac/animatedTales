A Greek musician, a Japanese god and seven Cherokee men all went into the land of the dead to bring someone back. Each was given a rule — and each of them broke it.

Start with the one you know. Orpheus, whose lyre could move even stones, lost his wife Eurydice to a snake. In the underworld, he played for the king and queen of the dead, and they were moved. She could follow him back, they said, on one condition: he must not look back until they had both reached the upper world.

Within sight of the daylight, he turned. She was taken back.

The Japanese tell it about a god, in the Kojiki, a book finished in the year 712.

Izanami had died giving birth, and her husband, Izanagi, followed her to the land of the dead. The world they had made was not finished, he told her. Come back.

It was too late, she said. She had already eaten the food of that land. But she would ask its gods. "Look not at me," she said, and went inside.

He waited. At last Izanagi broke a tooth from his comb, lit it like a torch, and went in.

Maggots swarmed over her body, and eight gods of thunder lived in it.

He ran. "You have shamed me," she said, and sent the dead after him. He threw down his headdress, and it turned into grapes. He threw down his comb, and it became bamboo shoots. At the foot of the pass, three peaches put the rest of them to flight.

Then Izanami came herself. Izanagi blocked the pass with a boulder that would take a thousand men to lift, and across it they spoke. If he did this, she said, every day she would strangle a thousand of his people. Then every day, he answered, he would build fifteen hundred birth huts.

So, in this story, people die every day. And every day, children are born.

On another continent, the Cherokee tell it about the daughter of the Sun. James Mooney published it in 1900 under the heading "Origin of death."

The Sun's heat killed the people with fever. They tried to kill her, and a rattlesnake bit her daughter instead. The Sun wept, and the world went dark.

The people were told to bring her back from the Ghost country. Seven men went, carrying a box. They found the dead dancing, struck the Sun's daughter down as she passed, and shut her in the box. Their one rule: do not open it, not even a little way, until you are home.

On the road she woke. She asked for food. For water. For air. They were afraid she was dying, and they lifted the lid a little. Something fluttered out into the thicket. A redbird. When they reached the settlements, the box was empty.

Three stories, three rules: do not look at her, do not look back, do not open the box. None of the three was broken out of carelessness. Izanagi could not wait. Orpheus could not bear not to see her. The Cherokee men were afraid she was dying.

Folklorists have recorded this tale among many Native American peoples. Why? Nobody knows. A few think it is very old, carried to America by the first people who crossed from Asia, though others doubt any myth can be traced so far. One Swedish scholar argued that such tales grew from the journeys of shamans into the land of the dead. And some think no one had to pass it on at all. Every people has lost someone. Every people has asked why they do not come back.

The Cherokee tale ends with its own answer. If the men had kept the box closed, we could bring back our friends from the Ghost country. But now, when they die, we can never bring them back.
