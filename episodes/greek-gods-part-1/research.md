# Research — Greek Gods, Part 1: Zeus, Poseidon, Hades (stage 03)

SERIES POSITION
Part 1 of 4. Spine, title rules, locked descriptions, the outro chain and the material boundaries
against Parts 2–4 live in `docs/series/greek-gods.md`. This file researches **only Part 1**.

TOPIC
The three sons of Cronus and the division of the universe between them.

RECOMMENDED STORY
Three brothers win a ten-year war against their father, and then have to decide who owns what.
They do not fight over it — they draw lots. One of them draws the dark, and ends up with the
richest kingdom of the three.

STORY ANGLE
Not "here are three Greek gods". The universe was not designed and not conquered — it was
**allotted**, by chance, in one throw. And the single most important piece of it was deliberately
left out of the draw: the earth, and Olympus, stayed common to all three. Every quarrel in Greek
mythology afterwards happens on the ground nobody won.

ONE-SENTENCE HOOK
Three brothers shook three lots in a helmet, and whatever each one drew, he would rule forever.

HISTORICAL PERIOD
No historical date — this is myth. Textual anchors: Hesiod's *Theogony*, c. 700 BCE; Homer's
*Iliad*, c. 750–700 BCE; Apollodorus' *Bibliotheca*, 1st–2nd c. CE; cult practice attested from the
archaic period through Pausanias (2nd c. CE).

LOCATION / CULTURAL CONTEXT
Archaic and classical Greek religion. Named places in the material: Crete (the birth cave), Mount
Olympus, Tartarus, the sanctuary of Hades at Elis, the omphalos stone at Delphi.

STORY TYPE
Myth / religion — World → Figure → Origin → Role → Conflict → Main story → Meaning.

KEY CHARACTERS
Cronus (the father who swallows); Rhea (the mother who hides one child); Zeus; Poseidon; Hades.
Non-speaking but necessary: the five swallowed siblings, the Cyclopes, the Hundred-Handers.

CENTRAL STORY QUESTION
Who ended up with what, and why did the one who drew worst end up richest?

CORE CONFLICT
First: children against a father who eats them. Then, after the win: three equals with one
universe and no agreement about who owns it.

TURNING POINT
The draw itself. Everything before it is a war story; everything after it is a story about what
each brother's share turned out to mean — and about the one piece they never divided.

PAYOFF
Hades drew the worst share and became "the rich one". Everything that comes out of the ground —
silver, gold, and the seed corn that goes under the soil and comes back up — belongs to his
kingdom, and every person who ever lives eventually arrives in it. The brother nobody envied owns
the only share that is guaranteed to keep growing.

KEY EVENTS

1. Gaia and Uranus tell Cronus he is fated to be overcome by his own son, as he overcame his own
   father. [TRADITION — Hesiod, *Theogony* 463–465]
2. Cronus swallows each child at birth: Hestia, Demeter, Hera, Hades, Poseidon.
   [TRADITION — *Theogony* 453–458]
3. Rhea, pregnant with the sixth, is hidden away by Gaia and gives birth in a cave on Crete.
   [TRADITION — *Theogony* 477–484. The mountain is named differently across sources — Aegaeum in
   Hesiod, elsewhere Ida or Dikte — so the script names Crete and not the mountain.]
4. She hands Cronus a stone wrapped in swaddling clothes; he swallows it without knowing.
   [TRADITION — *Theogony* 485–491]
5. Grown, Zeus forces his father to bring the children back up. The stone comes up first, then the
   five siblings, unharmed. [TRADITION — *Theogony* 492–500. The emetic administered by Metis is a
   later addition — Apollodorus 1.2.1 — and is **not used**: Metis belongs to Part 3.]
6. The Titan war lasts ten full years with neither side able to finish it.
   [TRADITION — *Theogony* 629–638]
7. Zeus frees the Cyclopes from the pit; in gratitude they forge weapons. In Hesiod they give the
   thunder and the lightning bolt; the full trio — thunderbolt for Zeus, trident for Poseidon, cap
   of invisibility for Hades — is Apollodorus.
   [TRADITION — *Theogony* 501–506 / Apollodorus 1.2.1]
8. Zeus also frees the three Hundred-Handers, who end the war by throwing three hundred rocks at
   once. [TRADITION — *Theogony* 713–719]
9. The Titans are thrown into Tartarus behind a bronze gate, with the Hundred-Handers as guards.
   [TRADITION — *Theogony* 717–735]
10. The three brothers draw lots. Poseidon gets the grey sea, Hades the misty dark, Zeus the wide
    sky — and the earth and Olympus are left **common to all three**.
    [TRADITION — Homer, *Iliad* 15.187–193, spoken by Poseidon himself]
11. Poseidon later claims equal rank with Zeus on the strength of that equal draw, refuses an
    order, and then backs down because Zeus is the elder.
    [TRADITION — *Iliad* 15.185–199, 204–205]
12. Hades is honoured almost nowhere: a single sanctuary at Elis, opened one day a year, which only
    the priest was permitted to enter. [RECORD — cult practice, Pausanias 6.25.2–3]
13. The Greeks avoid his name and call him **Plouton**, "the rich one" — Plato explains the name as
    the wealth that comes up out of the earth.
    [RECORD of texts — Plato, *Cratylus* 403a; the euphemism is standard in classical cult]

MOST INTERESTING FACTS

- **The lots were physical objects.** Homer describes Greek lot-drawing elsewhere: each man scratches
  his mark on a lot, the lots go into a helmet, someone shakes it until one jumps out
  (*Iliad* 7.175–183). The word used of the brothers' draw is the same shaking verb. This is why the
  storyboard can show a dented war helmet and three scratched pebbles without inventing anything.
- **Earth and Olympus were left out on purpose.** Poseidon states it as the terms of the deal. The
  gods divided everything except the one place where all their stories then take place.
- **The two great poems disagree about the division.** In Homer it is a lottery between equals. In
  Hesiod there is no lottery at all: after the war the gods *urge* Zeus to be king and he hands out
  the honours himself (*Theogony* 881–885). Two completely different political models.
- **Zeus is the elder brother and the youngest child.** Homer has Zeus claim seniority and Poseidon
  concede it (*Iliad* 15.165–167, 204–205). Hesiod lists Zeus last of the six. The poems never
  reconcile it.
- **The king's weapon was a gift from prisoners.** Zeus does not make the thunderbolt and does not
  win it — he is given it by the giants he releases.
- **Hades has no temples worth speaking of**, and the exception proves it: Elis, one day a year,
  one man allowed inside.
- **The stone was a real object people visited.** Pausanias saw the stone Cronus swallowed at
  Delphi; oil was poured over it every day and unspun wool laid on it at festivals
  (*Description of Greece* 10.24.6). [RECORD — cult practice]
- **Scale of the pit:** Hesiod measures Tartarus by saying a bronze anvil would fall nine days from
  heaven to earth, and nine more from earth to Tartarus (*Theogony* 722–725).

MYTHS / LEGENDS / TRADITIONS
The whole episode is tradition and is told as such — as a story inside Greek religion, not as
something that happened. The one place the narration steps outside the story is where the two
poems contradict each other, and where cult practice (Elis, Delphi, the name Plouton) shows what
Greeks actually did about these gods.

UNCERTAINTIES / DEBATES
- Lottery (Homer) vs. Zeus distributing honours (Hesiod). Genuinely unresolved; the script uses the
  lottery, attributes it to Homer by name, and does not pretend it is the only version.
- Whether Cronus or Uranus imprisoned the Cyclopes. Hesiod has Uranus bind them; Apollodorus has
  Cronus put them back. The script says only that they were prisoners in the pit.
- The trident and the cap of invisibility as a matched set with the thunderbolt is Apollodorus, not
  Hesiod. Kept, because it is the cleanest expression of the three shares — flagged here so it is
  not mistaken for Hesiod.
- "Plouton" as a euphemism for "Hades": the wealth etymology is ancient (Plato), but modern
  scholarship also treats Plouton as a partly separate agrarian figure absorbed into Hades. The
  script does not enter this.

AVOIDED ON PURPOSE
- **Athena and the contest for Athens.** Poseidon's most famous grievance is reserved for Part 3;
  Part 1 conveys the same thing through *Iliad* 15 and through "he lost cities to other gods", with
  no city and no rival named.
- **Metis, the swallowed wife, and Athena's birth.** Part 3's spine is that Zeus repeats his
  father's crime. Spending it here would cost Part 3 its callback.
- **Persephone.** Part 2. Part 1 says only that everyone eventually arrives in Hades' kingdom.
- Cronus castrating Uranus with the sickle — true, sourced, and one generation too far back for a
  four-minute episode.
- The Cretan details (Amaltheia the goat, the Kouretes clashing shields to hide the baby's crying).
  Excellent material, cut for time; first candidate if the episode runs short.

MATERIAL RESERVED FOR LATER PARTS
See `docs/series/greek-gods.md` §7. The binding ones for this script: no Athens contest, no Metis,
no Persephone, no Hephaestus, no marriage of Zeus and Hera.

ESSENTIAL MATERIAL
The prophecy and the swallowing; the stone; Zeus grown and the disgorging; the ten-year war; the
Cyclopes and the three weapons; the Hundred-Handers ending it; the draw and the three shares; earth
and Olympus left common; what each share actually meant — Zeus as the god of oaths and of the
arrangement itself, Poseidon as the brother who never accepted the result, Hades as the excluded
one; Plouton and the wealth of the ground; everyone arrives eventually.

OPTIONAL MATERIAL (cut first if long)
The anvil measuring Tartarus; the Delphi stone as a visitable object; the Elis sanctuary; the
Homer/Hesiod contradiction about who is the elder; Poseidon as god of horses and earthquakes.

STORY STRUCTURE
Beginning — the lots in the helmet, stated cold, before the viewer knows who is holding them.
Development — why there was anything to divide: the prophecy, the swallowing, the stone, the return.
Escalation — the ten-year war; the prisoners in the pit; the three weapons; the Hundred-Handers.
Turning point — the draw, and the clause that leaves the earth and Olympus out of it.
Payoff — the three shares examined one by one, ending on Hades: worst lot, richest kingdom,
guaranteed arrivals.

RECOMMENDED VOICE
Ancient / mysterious.

VOICE CHARACTER
Deep, atmospheric, deliberate, slightly dark. A narrator telling something old that is still in
force — not a showman, and not a lecturer.

VOICE DELIVERY
~145 wpm. Flat and unhurried through the swallowing; the list of five names spoken slowly, one beat
between each. Lift and open up at "the wide sky". Drop and slow for the whole Hades section, and
land the final two sentences quietly, without a push. Short pauses after the one-line paragraphs
("The child was Zeus." / "The rich one.").

WHY THIS VOICE FITS
The material is cosmogony — creation, inheritance, death and the division of the world. It does not
need the Commander archetype's intensity; it needs weight and patience, and a narrator who sounds
like the arrangement described is still the one we are living under. The payoff is quiet and ironic
rather than triumphant, and only a restrained voice can land it.
