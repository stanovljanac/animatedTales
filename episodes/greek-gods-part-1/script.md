Three brothers shook three lots in a helmet. Whatever each of them drew, he would rule it forever.

To understand how the universe came to be divided that way, start with their father.

Cronus ruled everything, and Cronus was afraid. He had been told that one of his children would overthrow him, exactly as he had overthrown his own father. So he found a solution. Every time his wife Rhea gave birth, he took the child and swallowed it.

Hestia. Demeter. Hera. Hades. Poseidon.

Five children, alive, inside him.

When the sixth was coming, Rhea hid. She gave birth in a cave on Crete, and handed her husband a stone wrapped in a baby's blanket. He swallowed it without looking.

The child was Zeus.

Grown, he came back and forced his father to bring up everything he had eaten. The stone came first. Then his brothers and sisters, one at a time, undamaged.

What followed was a war that lasted ten years, and neither side could finish it. So Zeus went down into the pit beneath the world and let out the prisoners kept there. Three one-eyed smiths made the weapons: a thunderbolt for Zeus, a trident for Poseidon, and for Hades a cap that made its wearer invisible. Three giants with a hundred arms each ended the fighting, and the Titans took their place in the pit.

And then the winners had a problem. They had won a universe, and no agreement about who owned it.

They did not fight over it. According to Homer, they drew lots.

Poseidon drew the sea. Hades drew the mist and the dark. Zeus drew the wide sky.

One part was left out of the draw on purpose. The earth, and Olympus itself, stayed common to all three. The place they all cared about most was the one place nobody won.

Zeus took the sky, and with it something more useful than weather. He became the god of the oath and of the stranger at the door — the rules that let strangers deal with each other at all. His weapon had been a gift from prisoners. His real power was the arrangement itself.

Poseidon drew more of the world's surface than either brother, and never accepted the result. In the Iliad he tells a messenger that they drew equally, that they are equals, and that Zeus has no right to give him orders. Then he backs down, because Zeus is the elder.

Which is strange. Hesiod has Zeus born last of the six. Neither poem explains the difference.

Hades drew worst. He got the dead, and a kingdom with no sky in it. He does not live on Olympus, and he is not one of the twelve who sit there. The Greeks were uneasy enough about him that they mostly avoided saying his name, and built him almost nothing under that name — one sanctuary, opened one day a year, with only the priest allowed inside.

What they called him instead was Plouton.

The rich one.

Because the dead were not all he had drawn. Everything that comes out of the ground comes out of his kingdom — the silver in the mines, the gold, and the seed that goes under the soil in autumn and comes back up in spring.

The brother who drew the darkness ended up holding the wealth of the world. And every person who has ever lived arrives, eventually, in his share of it.
