Three thousand years ago, an Egyptian court sentenced more than thirty people for a plot against their pharaoh. The record of their trial survives — and it never says what happened to the king.

The answer stayed hidden for three thousand years, on the king himself.

His name was Ramesses the Third, and the danger that reached him came from inside his own house.

A pharaoh's harem was not a pleasure garden but a household of its own, with officials and guards. One of the king's wives, Tiye, had a son, whom the record calls Pentawer. But the throne had been promised to another son.

Tiye set out to change that.

She found allies close to the king: the chief of his chamber, royal butlers, harem officials, a commander of the army. Messages went out to their families: "Stir up the people! Incite enemies to hostility against their lord."

They had magic, too: wax figures, made with a magic roll that belonged to Ramesses the Third himself, and smuggled into the harem to bewitch the guards. The king's own magic, turned against him.

What happened in the palace, the record never says. We know only the end: the king dead, his chosen son on the throne, the plotters on trial.

And the record opens in the king's own voice. Let all they have done fall upon their own heads, he says, "while I am among the just kings who are before Amun-Ra, king of gods, and before Osiris, ruler of eternity." He speaks like a man already among the dead.

The accused lost even their names. One butler went into the record as "Ra hates him", another as "Wicked in Thebes". To an Egyptian, a name was part of the soul.

Twenty-two were condemned in the first trial. Six more were left to take their own lives in the court. Then came the prince: "Pentawer, who bore that other name." Even Pentawer was not his real name. "They found him guilty. They left him in his place. He took his own life."

Then the court itself went on trial. Some of the accused women had visited two of the judges at home and, the papyrus says, "made a beer-hall" with them. Both judges lost their noses and ears. One of them then took his own life.

Of Tiye's own trial, nothing survives. Her fate is unknown.

For more than a century, historians argued over whether the king had been murdered at all. His mummy, found in 1881 in a hidden tomb of royal mummies, showed no wound. Only a thick collar of linen around the neck.

In 2012, researchers put him through a CT scanner. Under the linen, his throat had been cut, nearly three inches wide, through the windpipe and down to the bones of the spine. Inside the wound sat an Eye of Horus amulet, most likely placed there by the embalmers, to heal him for the next life.

So the plot had worked. The king was dead. And it had failed. The son it was meant to crown never ruled.

In the same hidden tomb lay another body: a young man, eighteen to twenty, in a coffin with no name. He was wrapped in a goatskin, unclean to Egyptians, his mouth hanging open as if he were screaming. He was catalogued as Unknown Man E.

DNA showed he shared the king's male line, most likely as a son. The researchers named him a possible candidate for Pentawer.

In 2021, Egypt moved its royal mummies to a new museum, in a golden parade through Cairo. Ramesses the Third rode in it. Right behind him came Ramesses the Fourth, the son the plotters had tried to push aside.

The young man in the goatskin was not among them. He still has no name. Only a label: Unknown Man E.
