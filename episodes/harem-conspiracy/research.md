# Research — The Harem Conspiracy (stage 03)

TOPIC
New idea #31 in `docs/ideas.md` (added 2026-10-18, outside the original thirty): the plot inside
the royal harem against Ramesses III, c. 1155 BCE — the trial, its silences, and the 2012 CT and
DNA study that answered what the trial record never says.

RECOMMENDED STORY
A secondary wife of Ramesses III plots to kill the king and put her son on the throne instead of
the chosen heir. She recruits the king's own household; wax figures made from the king's own magic
roll are smuggled into the harem. The plotters are tried and condemned — more than thirty of them —
and two of the judges are themselves mutilated for partying with the accused women. But the trial
record never says what happened to the king. For a century historians argued over whether he was
murdered at all. In 2012 a CT scan found his throat cut to the spine under a linen collar, and DNA
linked an unnamed, goatskin-wrapped "screaming mummy" from the same tomb to him as a likely son.

STORY ANGLE
The record's silences. The court wrote down names, crimes, verdicts and even a drinking party — and
left out the one thing the viewer wants to know. The angle is a document that refuses to answer,
answered three thousand years later by the body itself. The second silence is the prince's name:
the court took it, and it has never come back. Both payoffs are literal, not rhetorical.

ONE-SENTENCE HOOK (verbatim from ideas.md)
Three thousand years ago, an Egyptian court sentenced more than thirty people for a plot against
their pharaoh. The record of their trial survives — and it never says what happened to the king.

HISTORICAL PERIOD
New Kingdom, 20th Dynasty. Ramesses III reigned c. 1186–1155 BCE (conventional; other schemes shift
this by one to three years — Wikipedia gives 1185–1154). He died in regnal year 32, III Shemu 15
(Wikipedia, Ramesses III). The trials were held in the same year, under his son Ramesses IV. The
CT/DNA study: published in the BMJ, December 2012. Toe finding: 2016. Golden Parade: 3 April 2021.

LOCATION / CULTURAL CONTEXT
Thebes and the royal residence; the palace beside Ramesses III's temple at Medinet Habu is the usual
assumption for where the attack happened, but no source places it there (see UNCERTAINTIES). The
mummies: the Royal Cache (DB320) at Deir el-Bahari, found 1881. Ramesses III is in the National
Museum of Egyptian Civilization since the 2021 parade; Unknown Man E was exhibited at the Egyptian
Museum, Tahrir, in 2018 — where he is now is not verified.

STORY TYPE
Historical event told as a mystery — Question → Evidence → Competing explanations → What we know →
What remains unknown, with the event chain (Situation → Trigger → Escalation → Trial → Consequences)
inside it.

KEY CHARACTERS
- **Ramesses III** — the king; the last great warrior pharaoh of the New Kingdom; the victim.
- **Tiye** — one of his wives, generally a minor or secondary wife; organises the plot to crown her
  son. Her trial and fate are not in the surviving records.
- **Pentawer** — her son, the intended beneficiary. "Pentawer" is a name the court gave him; his
  real name is unknown. Forced to take his own life.
- **The chief of the chamber** (Pebekkamen in the records) — the arch-conspirator inside the
  household; carries the women's messages out. Unnamed in the script on purpose.
- **The two corrupted judges** (Pebes and Mai) — unnamed in the script.
- **Ramesses IV** — the designated heir who takes the throne; named only at the end.
- **Unknown Man E** — the young man in the goatskin; a possible Pentawer.
Unnamed in the script on purpose: Mesedsure and Binemwese (given only as the meanings of their court
names), Peyes the army commander, Penhuibin the overseer of herds, the twelve judges, Hori.

CENTRAL STORY QUESTION
What happened to the king — and what happened to the people who did it?

CORE CONFLICT
A wife and her son against the king and his chosen heir, fought inside one household; then a court
that must judge the king's own servants, and cannot even keep its own judges clean.

TURNING POINT
The CT scan (2012). Until then the record's silence allowed every reading, including that the king
survived or that there was no attack at all. The cut under the linen collar ends the argument: the
plot killed him. The story's meaning flips from "a failed coup" to "a murder that worked and a coup
that failed".

PAYOFF
The plot worked — the king was dead — and failed: the son it was meant to crown never ruled. In the
same hidden tomb lay a young man wrapped in a ritually unclean goatskin, in a coffin with no name,
who shares the king's male line: a possible Pentawer. In 2021 Ramesses III rode through Cairo in the
Golden Parade with Ramesses IV directly behind him; the young man in the goatskin was not among
them. He still has no name — even "Pentawer" was never his — only a label: Unknown Man E.

KEY EVENTS

Primary texts are quoted from J. H. Breasted, *Ancient Records of Egypt* IV (1906), §§416–456 — the
Judicial Papyrus of Turin (JPT) and Papyrus Lee + Papyrus Rollin (one manuscript, three leaves).
Breasted is old but complete; the quotations in the script are his wording. Category in brackets:
HR = historical record, TR = tradition/belief, ID = interpretation/debate.

1. **The household.** Tiye "plots to make way with the old king and to place her son Pentewere upon
   the throne" (Breasted §416). The throne had been designated for another son, the future Ramesses
   IV (Wikipedia, JPT: "a son of another wife, Tyti" — his mother is debated). [HR]
2. **The recruits.** Breasted's count from the JPT (§417): ten harem officials of various ranks,
   four royal butlers, an overseer of the treasury, a captain of archers in Nubia, an army commander
   (Peyes), three royal scribes, Pebekkamen's assistant and others, plus six wives of the men of the
   harem gate. "As most of these people were in the personal service of the Pharaoh, the dangerous
   character of the complot is evident." [HR]
3. **The messages out.** Pebekkamen, "formerly chief of the chamber … began bringing out their words
   to their mothers and their brothers who were there, saying: 'Stir up the people! Incite enemies
   to hostility against their lord.'" (§427). Binemwese's sister in the harem wrote to him: "Incite
   the people to hostility! And come thou to begin hostility against thy lord." (§443). [HR]
   Breasted's reading: "a revolution outside of the palace was intended to accompany their own coup
   within it" (§417). [ID — the script says nothing beyond the quoted message.]
4. **The magic.** Papyrus Rollin: an accused whose name is lost "began to make magic rolls for
   hindering and terrifying, and to make some gods of wax, and some people, for enfeebling the limbs
   of people; and gave them into the hand of Pebekkamen … saying: 'Take them in;' and they took them
   in" (§454). Papyrus Lee: Penhuibin, "formerly overseer of herds", asked for "a roll for enduing me
   with strength and might", was given "a magic roll of Usermare-Meriamon (Ramses III) … the Great
   God, his lord", and "began to make people of wax, inscribed, in order that they might be taken in
   by the inspector, Errem, hindering one troop and bewitching the others, that a few words might be
   taken in, and others brought out" (§455). He was executed. [HR that this was charged and judged;
   TR that wax figures could bewitch — the script frames it as the plotters' weapon.]
5. **The attack.** "That the latter involved the assassination of the king is nowhere stated"
   (Breasted §417). No surviving text describes the attack, the place or the night. [HR — the
   silence itself is the fact.]
6. **The end state.** Ramesses III died in year 32; Ramesses IV, the designated heir, succeeded; the
   trials took place the same year (Wikipedia, Ramesses III / JPT). [HR]
7. **The king's voice.** The JPT opens in the name of "Usermare-Meriamun … Ruler of Heliopolis". He
   commissions twelve named officials (two overseers of the treasury, two standard-bearers, five
   butlers, a herald, two scribes; Breasted counts fourteen across all hearings, §419) and says: "As
   for the words which the people have spoken, I know them not. Go ye and examine them. … they shall
   cause to die by their own hand, those who should die, without [my] knowing it. … as for all that
   has been done, and those who have done it, let all that they have done fall upon their (own)
   heads; while I am protected and defended forever, while I am among the just kings, who are before
   Amon-Re, king of gods, and before Osiris, ruler of eternity." (§§423–424). Breasted glosses the
   last line: "that is, while I am among my deceased fathers" (§418). [HR text; ID on who really
   spoke — see UNCERTAINTIES.]
8. **"The Great God".** Papyrus Lee calls Ramesses III "the Great God", "applied at this time only
   to deceased kings" (Breasted §418 note, §455 note). So the record was written after his death.
   [HR / standard reading]
9. **The court names.** "The chief conspirators are given fictitious names … Mesedsure means 'Re
   hates him,' and Binemwese is 'Wicked in Thebes.' Pentewere … is not a term of opprobrium, but is
   not his real name" (§421). Mesedsure was "formerly butler" (§428); Binemwese was the captain of
   archers in Nubia (§443). [HR] For Egyptians the name (*ren*) was a part of the person that had to
   survive for the person to survive in the afterlife. [TR — framed as "to an Egyptian".]
10. **First prosecution.** Six judges; condemned, by my count of the entries in §§427–443: fourteen
    named men, the six wives of the harem-gate men, Pere the overseer of the treasury and Binemwese —
    **22**, which matches Breasted §420. "They examined them; they found them guilty; they brought
    their punishment upon them; their crimes seized them." The penalty is not named; "it was certainly
    death" (Breasted). [HR; penalty ID]
11. **Second prosecution.** Six, including Peyes, commander of the army: "they left them in their own
    hands in the court of examination; they took their own lives; and no punishment was executed upon
    them." (§444–445). [HR]
12. **Third prosecution.** Four, before butlers. "Pentewere, who bore that other name. He was brought
    in because of his collusion [with] Tiy, his mother … He was placed before the butlers, in order
    to examine him; they found him guilty; they left him in his place; he took his own life."
    (§447). [HR]
13. **The judges.** "Persons upon whom punishment was executed by cutting off their noses and their
    ears, because of their forsaking the good testimony delivered to them. The women had gone; had
    arrived at their place of abode, and had there caroused with them and with Peyes." "Caroused" is
    literally "made a beer-hall" (§451 and note). Punished: the judges Pebes (butler) and Mai
    (scribe), and two officers, Teynakhte (infantry) and Oneney (police). "Pebes … This punishment
    was executed upon him; he was left (alone); he took his own life." (§452). Hori, a
    standard-bearer, "was dismissed; punishment was not executed upon him" (§453). [HR]
14. **Tiye.** "The fate of the queen, Tiy, is unknown, as the records preserved do not contain her
    trial." (§420). [HR — the silence]
15. **The long argument.** The JPT was published by Devéria, 1865–68 (Breasted §416 note). Breasted
    (1906) held that the king probably escaped unhurt and died soon after (§418). The mummy, found in
    the Royal Cache (DB320) in 1881, "appeared to show no outward signs of any injuries and his death
    was presumed to be a natural one" (Wikipedia, Harem conspiracy, citing Harris & Weeks 1973). The
    neck "was covered by a collar of thick linen layers" (BMJ 2012 abstract). [HR]
16. **The CT scan (2012).** Hawass et al., BMJ 2012;345:e8268: a wound "directly under the larynx …
    roughly 70 mm wide and extended to the bones (fifth to seventh cervical vertebra), severing all
    soft tissue areas in the anterior side of the neck. The trachea was clearly cut…"; "could have
    caused immediate death". A Horus eye (wedjat) amulet, ~15 mm, lay in the wound, "most probably
    inserted by the ancient Egyptian embalmers during the mummification process to promote healing".
    Conclusion: "Ramesses III was murdered during the harem conspiracy by the cutting of his throat."
    [HR — physical evidence; "most probably" kept in the script as "most likely".]
17. **The toe (2016).** Hawass & Saleem, *Scanning the Pharaohs*: part of a big toe was chopped off
    and never healed; embalmers made a replacement of linen and resin; "likely several assailants
    using different weapons simultaneously attacked the king" (via History.com). [ID] — cut from the
    script for time.
18. **Unknown Man E.** Found in the same cache; unwrapped 1886. Aged 18–20; "covered with a
    'ritually impure' goatskin"; "not mummified in the usual way"; "no evidence that the man's
    internal organs or brain were removed" (World Archaeology); uninscribed coffin (Wikipedia);
    "contorted expression" noted at the 1886 unwrapping (AuntMinnie caption) — popularly "the
    screaming mummy". Inflated thorax and compressed skin folds around the neck "could suggest violent
    actions such as strangulation … though these effects could also be influenced by decomposition"
    (World Archaeology). [HR; strangulation ID — cut from the script.]
19. **DNA.** Identical Y-chromosome haplotypes, "a common male lineage"; "strongly suggesting that
    they were father and son" (AuntMinnie). "Unknown man E is a possible candidate as Ramesses III's
    son Pentawere" (BMJ abstract). World Archaeology: "it is not possible to establish which of
    Ramesses' many sons this could be." [HR for the match; ID for the identity — the script says
    "possible candidate".]
20. **The Golden Parade.** 3 April 2021, 22 royal mummies moved from the Egyptian Museum (Tahrir) to
    the National Museum of Egyptian Civilization (Fustat), carriages "in chronological order of their
    reigns": no. 18 Ramesses III, no. 19 Ramesses IV (Wikipedia, Pharaohs' Golden Parade). The
    parade carried 18 kings and 4 queens; Unknown Man E was not among them. [HR]

CAUSES
- A succession dispute inside the royal household: Tiye wanted her son on the throne instead of the
  designated heir.
- Access: the plotters were the king's own servants — chamber, butlers, harem staff, gate officers.

CONSEQUENCES
- The king died; the coup failed; Ramesses IV reigned.
- More than thirty people condemned, most by execution or forced suicide; judges mutilated.
- The accused were written into the record under curse-names; the prince is known only by a name the
  court gave him.

MOST INTERESTING FACTS
- The trial record never says the king was attacked, or how he died.
- The king's speech is written as if he is already among the dead kings.
- The wax figures were made with a magic roll belonging to Ramesses III himself.
- Two judges partied with the accused women — the papyrus literally says they "made a beer-hall".
- Court curse-names: "Ra hates him", "Wicked in Thebes". "Pentawer" is not the prince's real name.
- An Eye of Horus amulet sat inside the king's wound — placed to heal him for the afterlife.
- The possible prince was buried in a goatskin, ritually unclean, in a coffin with no name.
- In the 2021 parade Ramesses IV rode directly behind his father.

MYTHS / LEGENDS / TRADITIONS
- Wax-figure magic and the power of a name are Egyptian belief, not effective weapons; the script
  frames them through the plotters and "to an Egyptian".
- "The screaming mummy": the open mouth is popular framing. Maspero's 1886 theory (poisoning or
  burial alive) is not supported and is not used; an open jaw is common in mummies.

UNCERTAINTIES / DEBATES
- **Who spoke the commission?** Breasted (1906) believed the living king issued it and survived the
  plot. The modern consensus (Wikipedia JPT: "It is now known that Ramesses III did not survive …
  the trial was carried out by his successor Ramesses IV in the name of his murdered father"). The
  script says the record "opens in the king's own voice" and "speaks like a man already among the
  dead" — true under both readings.
- **Where and when.** Wikipedia places the attempt in the harem of the Western/High Gate at Medinet
  Habu during the Beautiful Festival of the Valley; another source (gurob.org.uk) names the Opet
  Festival. Not used in the script.
- **Dates.** 1155 vs 1154 BCE; the Harem article gives "day 15 of month 2 of Shemu" against the
  Ramesses III article's "III Shemu 15". The script uses no date before 1881.
- **Counts.** Breasted's prosecutions: 22 + 6 + 4 condemned, 4 mutilated, 1 dismissed. Wikipedia:
  "28 people were executed while 10, including Pentawer, were allowed to take their own lives". Sahar
  Saleem (2019): "31 conspirators were sentenced to death". The script uses only "more than thirty",
  "twenty-two" (the first prosecution, countable in the text) and "six more".
- **The toe.** History.com says the right big toe; the script does not use it.
- **Goatskin or sheepskin.** The BMJ says goatskin; a 2018 ministry statement said sheepskin. The
  script follows the BMJ.
- **Man E's identity.** "Possible candidate" — not proven. DNA cannot tell which son.
- **Tiye's status.** Usually a minor or secondary wife; Breasted thought she might be the king's
  mother or stepmother. The script says only "one of the king's wives".

STORY STRUCTURE
Beginning — the record that will not answer (hook) → who the king was and where the danger came
from.
Development — Tiye, the son, the promised throne; allies, messages, wax figures.
Escalation — the silence about the attack; the court in the king's voice; names taken; verdicts;
the prince's suicide; the judges' beer-hall; Tiye's fate unknown.
Turning point — a century of argument; the linen collar; the CT scan and the Eye of Horus.
Payoff — the plot worked and failed; the second body in the same tomb; DNA; the 2021 parade; "He
still has no name. Only a label: Unknown Man E."

RECOMMENDED VOICE
Dark / dramatic.

VOICE CHARACTER
Serious, restrained, emotionally controlled — a crime told by someone who has read the file and does
not need to raise his voice.

VOICE DELIVERY
~145 wpm. The papyrus quotations read flat and formal, like a clerk reading a docket — their
coldness is the effect. The king's speech slower, distant. A pause after "He took his own life." The
CT section quiet and clinical. Slow for "So the plot had worked", and land the last three short
sentences with no push.

WHY THIS VOICE FITS
It is an assassination and a betrayal inside a family, told largely through court records and a
medical scan. Theatricality would cheapen both; restraint lets the details (the beer-hall, the eye
in the wound, the goatskin) do the work.
