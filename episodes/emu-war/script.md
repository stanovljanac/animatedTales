Australia sent soldiers with machine guns to defeat a bird. The birds won.

In 1932, farmers in Western Australia were in trouble. After the First World War, many veterans had been given land to farm, often poor land. Then came the Depression, and the price of wheat kept falling, despite the government's promised help.

And then came the emus. By one count there were as many as twenty thousand, moving across the wheat country around Campion and wrecking the crops and the fences.

The farmers were veterans. They knew what a machine gun could do. A group of them went to the Minister of Defence, Sir George Pearce, and asked for some. He agreed, on conditions: soldiers would work the guns, the state would pay for their transport, and the farmers would pay for the ammunition and food.

On the second of November, Major Meredith of the Royal Australian Artillery arrived at Campion with two soldiers, two Lewis guns, and ten thousand rounds. The first emus were sighted at once, about fifty of them, out of range. The farmers tried to drive them into an ambush, but the birds split into small groups and scattered.

Two days later, Meredith set up near a dam, and more than a thousand emus walked toward him. The gun jammed after twelve birds. A gun mounted on a truck was no use either, because the truck could not catch the birds.

By the eighth of November, six days in, the soldiers had fired twenty-five hundred rounds. Estimates of the birds killed run from fifty to five hundred. Meredith's men had suffered no casualties, he reported, except for their dignity. That week, one member of Parliament asked, mockingly, whether a medal would be struck for the soldiers. On the eighth, Pearce pulled the troops out.

The emus kept eating, and the Premier of Western Australia asked for help. On the thirteenth, the guns went back. The first two days brought about forty emus. By the beginning of December, the soldiers were killing about a hundred a week. On the tenth, Meredith was recalled.

His report claimed 986 birds killed with 9,860 rounds, exactly ten rounds for each one. He added that another 2,500 had died of their wounds. A historian, Greg Beyer, calls the figure highly disputed and probably very inflated. And Meredith is quoted as saying that a military division with the bullet-carrying capacity of these birds would face any army in the world.

The army never came back. The farmers asked again in 1934, 1943 and 1948, and each time were refused. What worked was the bounty the state had begun in 1923. In six months of 1934, farmers claimed 57,034 bounties.

So who won? The press called it the Emu War, and a later newspaper called the army's retreat the bird's most complete victory. A paper in 1935 said the machine guns had worked after all. What is clear is that it took a bounty to do what the guns could not.
