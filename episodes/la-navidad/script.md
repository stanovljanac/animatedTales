The first Spanish settlement in the Americas was built from a shipwreck. Within a year, nobody who lived in it was alive.

By December of 1492, Christopher Columbus had spent ten weeks among the islands of the Caribbean. One of his three ships, the Pinta, had sailed off on its own weeks earlier and had not come back. He was left with two: his flagship, the Santa María, and the small Niña. On the twenty-second of December, they reached a large island he called Hispaniola, where a chief named Guacanagarí invited the ships to his town.

Columbus's men went ahead in boats. They found a town of more than two thousand men, with a square swept clean, where every inhabitant brought them something to eat or drink. When the Spaniards wanted to leave that afternoon, the chief asked them to stay another day.

The next night was Christmas Eve. The Santa María was sailing along the coast in a dead calm. Columbus had not slept for two days and a night. A little before midnight, he went below to rest. The helmsman decided to do the same, and left the tiller to a ship's boy, which Columbus had forbidden for the whole voyage.

The current carried the ship onto a sandbank so gently that nobody felt it. Only the boy, who heard the surf, cried out. Columbus ordered the master, Juan de la Cosa, to row out an anchor and pull the ship off. Instead, the master and some of the crew took the boat toward the Niña, to save themselves. The Niña refused them. By then the Santa María had fallen on her side, and her timbers opened.

Guacanagarí heard the news and, Columbus wrote, wept. He sent canoes, and his people emptied the ship in a few hours. Not even a needle was missing. The chief gave the Spaniards two large houses. Soon he was telling Columbus, by signs, that there was a great deal of gold on the island, in a place called Cibao. He also told him about the Caribs, enemies who raided his people. Columbus answered that the sovereigns of Castile would have the Caribs destroyed.

On the thirtieth of December, five lesser chiefs arrived, each wearing a crown. Guacanagarí took off his own crown and put it on Columbus's head. Columbus gave him a collar of colored beads, a fine cloak, a pair of colored boots and a silver ring. The chief, Columbus wrote, was delighted.

By then, Columbus had decided that the disaster was a piece of good fortune. If the ship had not sunk, he wrote, he would have sailed on and left nobody behind. Now he had a single ship, and it did not seem prudent to explore any further with it. Many of his men were asking to stay, hoping to find the source of the gold. So he ordered a tower and a fort built from the wreck, and called the place La Navidad, after the day the ship was lost. He wrote that the people of the island were naked, unarmed and timid, and that he could subjugate all of it with the men he had.

On the second of January, Columbus went ashore to say goodbye. He had a cannon fired through the hull of the wreck, and ordered his fully armed crews to stage a skirmish. This was done, he wrote, so that the chief would look on the men he was leaving as friends, and also have a proper fear of them. Guacanagarí showed great sorrow at his departure. A relative of the chief told Columbus that a statue of pure gold, as big as the Admiral, had been ordered, and would be ready in ten days.

Columbus left thirty-nine men, by his own count, with a year of bread and wine, goods for trade, artillery, and the ship's boat. A list drawn up later has forty-four names, among them an Irishman and an Englishman. There was a carpenter, a gunner, a doctor, a tailor. Three men were put in command. Their task was to find where the gold came from. Two days later, Columbus sailed for Spain.

He came back in November, with seventeen ships. On the night of the twenty-seventh, he had two cannons fired, expecting the fort to answer. Nothing did. On the shore there was no one, and no sign of houses. La Navidad had burned to the ground, and some of the men lay dead in the fields.

Guacanagarí, in his hammock, told the Spaniards what he said had happened. Some of the men had died of disease. Some had gone to the lands of another chief, Caonabó, looking for gold, and were killed there. The rest were attacked in their houses. Later Spanish accounts, built on what the Taíno told them, add that the men had quarreled among themselves, each taking as much gold and as many women as he could.

That is all anyone has. The Taíno left no written account of those eleven months, and every version comes through Spanish pens. Whether the chief who had welcomed them had a hand in it, historians still ask. Columbus himself had written in December that he suspected the chief of steering him away from the sources of the gold, so that the Spaniards would stay in his town. Each side, it seems, wanted something from the other: Columbus, gold, and the chief, an ally against his enemies.

And nobody has found the fort. At En Bas Saline, thought to be Guacanagarí's town, excavators uncovered a large burned structure on a mound that was already standing in 1492, with Spanish pottery, a lead musket ball, and the bones of rats and pigs. The Florida Museum calls it the most likely candidate. It has not been proven. One historian now suggests that the coast has moved so far since 1492 that the wreck may lie under some six metres of earth.

Columbus called the loss of his ship good fortune. The fort was built from its timbers, and still no one can point to a single one of them beyond dispute.
