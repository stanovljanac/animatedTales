# Research — Greek Gods, Part 3: Athena, Apollo, Artemis (stage 03)

SERIES POSITION
Part 3 of 4. Spine, locked descriptions, the outro chain and the material boundaries against
Parts 1, 2 and 4 live in `docs/series/greek-gods.md`. This file researches **only Part 3**.
Part 1 told the swallowing and the draw; Part 2 told the sisters and named Hera's pursuit of
Zeus's lovers as a pattern. Part 3 is the series' return to Part 1: the prophecy that made Cronus
swallow his children comes back, and this time it is Zeus who swallows.

TOPIC
The births of Athena, Apollo and Artemis — three children someone in Zeus's family tried to stop.

RECOMMENDED STORY
Zeus, who overthrew a father for swallowing his children, hears the same prophecy about his own
wife and swallows her while she is pregnant. The daughter is born anyway, out of his head, and
becomes his favourite. Hera, who cannot touch a child born from her husband's head, hunts the next
mother across the earth so that no land will let her give birth. The only place that takes Leto in
is a drifting rock, and the twins born there become the god of prophecy and the goddess women call
on in childbirth.

STORY ANGLE
"Never supposed to be born" — Part 2's outro promised it. The angle is that every attempt in this
family to stop a child fails, and that each child grows into the very thing that was used against
its birth: the daughter of the swallowed wife is trusted with Zeus's thunderbolt; the child of the
mother no land would accept becomes the god every city asks before it acts; the girl born where
birth was forbidden becomes the goddess of birth. The one child who is successfully stopped is the
son who would have been king — and his mother is still inside Zeus.

ONE-SENTENCE HOOK
Zeus overthrew a father who swallowed his children — then heard the same prophecy, and swallowed
his wife.

HISTORICAL PERIOD
Myth, no date. Textual anchors: Hesiod, *Theogony* (c. 700 BCE); Homer, *Iliad* (c. 750–700 BCE);
*Homeric Hymn to Apollo* (Delian part 7th–6th c. BCE; the whole poem stood by 523 BCE);
*Homeric Hymn* 28 *to Athena*; Pindar, *Olympian* 7 (464 BCE) and fr. 33c–d; Aeschylus, *Eumenides*
(458 BCE); Herodotus (5th c. BCE); Thucydides (late 5th c. BCE); Callimachus, *Hymns* 3 and 4
(3rd c. BCE); Apollodorus, *Library* (1st–2nd c. CE); Pausanias (2nd c. CE). Record anchors: the
Athenian purification of Delos, 426/5 BCE; the Persian burning of the Acropolis, 480 BCE; the
Delphic oracle, active from the 8th c. BCE to the late 4th c. CE.

LOCATION / CULTURAL CONTEXT
Archaic and classical Greek religion. Named places: Olympus, Athens and its Acropolis, Delos,
Delphi.

STORY TYPE
Myth / religion — World → Figure → Origin → Role → Conflict → Main story → Meaning, told as one
conflict (the attempt to stop a birth) that happens twice.

KEY CHARACTERS
Zeus, Metis, Athena, Hera, Leto, Apollo, Artemis. Present in one line each: Poseidon (the contest
for Athens), Cronus (recalled, not seen acting), the goddess of childbirth (Eileithyia, unnamed in
the script), Earth and Sky (Gaia and Ouranos, the advisers).

CENTRAL STORY QUESTION
Can anyone in this family stop a child from being born?

CORE CONFLICT
Births against the people trying to prevent them: Zeus against the prophecy of a son, Hera against
her husband's children by other women.

TURNING POINT
The bribe reaches the goddess of childbirth and she sets foot on Delos. Until then the story is a
chain of refusals — the swallowed wife, the lands that turn Leto away, Hera holding back the one
goddess a birth needs. From that moment the children arrive and the story turns to what they
became.

PAYOFF
None of the three was supposed to be born. The daughter of the swallowed wife ends up with the keys
to her father's thunderbolt; the twins from the drifting rock become the goddess of birth and the
voice of prophecy, and Apollo's sanctuary keeps the stone Cronus swallowed — the object where the
whole series began. The only child who never came is the son who would have been king. His mother
is still inside Zeus.

**Metis — the prophecy comes back**
1. After the Titans are beaten and Zeus is made king, he takes Metis as his first wife; Hesiod calls
   her the one who knows most among gods and mortal men. [TRADITION — *Theogony* 881–887]
2. When she is about to give birth to Athena, Zeus "deceives her wits with guile and coaxing words"
   and puts her into his belly, on the advice of Earth and starry Sky — the same pair who warned
   Cronus (*Theogony* 463–465). [TRADITION — *Theogony* 888–893]
3. The fate he is avoiding: Metis would bear first a daughter "equal to her father in strength and
   wise counsel", then a son, king of gods and men, with an overbearing heart. [TRADITION —
   *Theogony* 894–898] The script calls it "the same prophecy his father had heard": in both cases
   a son replaces the king. The substance is the same; the wording is not, and the script does not
   quote either.
4. Zeus keeps her inside him "so that the goddess might counsel him on good and evil". [TRADITION —
   *Theogony* 899–900] Later fragments (*Theogony* 929a–t, via Chrysippus in Galen) keep her there.

**Athena — born out of the king's head**
5. Zeus himself gives birth to Athena from his head. [TRADITION — *Theogony* 924–926]
6. She springs from his head in golden armour, shaking a sharp spear; Olympus reels, the earth
   cries out, the sea heaves with dark waves, and Helios stops his horses until she takes the armour
   off her shoulders. [TRADITION — *Homeric Hymn* 28.4–16]
7. The axe: Pindar has Hephaestus' bronze axe open the head, and Athena springs out "with a great
   shout"; Apollodorus says Prometheus or Hephaestus struck the blow. [TRADITION — Pindar,
   *Olympian* 7.35–38; Apollodorus 1.3.6] The script says only "later poets say it took an axe" and
   **does not name the wielder** — Hephaestus is Part 4's god.
8. Her mother's name, *mētis*, means cunning intelligence, practical wisdom. Athena's provinces —
   strategy in war, crafts and weaving (Athena Ergane), the protection of cities (Polias) — are the
   standard picture. [RECORD of cult titles + INTERPRETATION of the name, uncontested]
9. Ares complains to Zeus that he indulges this daughter "because you yourself gave birth to her".
   [TRADITION — *Iliad* 5.875–880] Supports "her father's favourite"; cut from the script as a line.
10. In Aeschylus Athena says: "I alone of the gods know the keys of the chamber where his
    thunderbolt is sealed." [TRADITION as drama — *Eumenides* 827–828] The script attributes it to
    "one Athenian tragedy", not to myth in general.

**Athena — the contest for Athens** (reserved for Part 3 by the series bible, §7)
11. The gods resolve to take cities in which each will be worshipped. Poseidon comes first to Attica,
    strikes the middle of the Acropolis with his trident and produces a "sea" (salt water); Athena
    plants an olive. The twelve gods judge and award the land to Athena; Poseidon, angry, floods the
    Thriasian plain and puts Attica under the sea. [TRADITION — Apollodorus 3.14.1] Other judges
    are reported (Cecrops; the citizens' vote is Varro, via Augustine) — the script says "the gods".
12. Callback: Part 1's script says Poseidon "never accepted the result" of the draw. Part 3 calls
    him "the brother who never accepted his share" — the series' own wording, not a new claim.
13. The Athenians kept both tokens on the Acropolis: an olive and a well of sea water. When the
    Persians burned the Acropolis (480 BCE), the olive burned; the next day a shoot about a cubit
    long had sprung from the stump. [RECORD of what Athenians showed and told — Herodotus 8.55;
    Pausanias 1.26.5, 1.27.2] Cut for time; see OPTIONAL.
14. The Parthenon's east pediment showed Athena's birth, the west pediment the contest with
    Poseidon. [RECORD — Pausanias 1.24.5; the surviving sculpture] Cut for time; see OPTIONAL.

**Leto — the mother no land would take**
15. Leto, daughter of the Titans Coeus and Phoebe, bears Apollo and Artemis to Zeus. [TRADITION —
    *Theogony* 404–408, 918–920] In Hesiod's list of Zeus's unions Leto comes **before** Hera; the
    *Hymn to Apollo* already has Hera as Zeus's jealous wife. The script tells the Hymn's version,
    as Part 2 did.
16. Hera hunts Leto over the whole earth. [TRADITION — Apollodorus 1.4.1; Callimachus, *Hymn* 4,
    has Hera forbid every land to receive her]
17. Leto wanders a long list of places — Crete, Athens, Aegina, Euboea and many more — and every one
    of them "trembled and feared"; none, not even the richest, dared receive the god. [TRADITION —
    *Hymn to Apollo* 30–48]
18. Delos as a floating island, tossed by winds and waves until Leto set foot on it and four pillars
    rose from the sea floor to fix it. [TRADITION — Pindar fr. 33c–d; Callimachus, *Hymn* 4] The
    script says "in some tellings".
19. Delos fears the god will scorn a hard, rocky island and "overturn it and thrust it down with his
    feet into the sea". Leto promises that men will bring hecatombs to his temple — riches the poor
    island could never grow — and swears by Earth, Sky and the Styx that his altar and precinct will
    stand there forever. [TRADITION — *Hymn to Apollo* 51–88]
20. Labour of nine days and nine nights. The greatest goddesses come — Dione, Rhea, Themis,
    Amphitrite and others — all except Hera. [TRADITION — *Hymn* 89–95]
21. Eileithyia, goddess of childbirth, alone has not heard: Hera keeps her on Olympus under golden
    clouds, out of jealousy, because Leto is about to bear a son "faultless and strong". [TRADITION —
    *Hymn* 97–101] The script names her role, not her name (one hard name fewer for the voice and
    for the viewer).
22. The goddesses send Iris to bring her secretly, promising a great necklace strung with golden
    threads, **nine cubits long**. [TRADITION — *Hymn* 102–114] The number stays in the script.
23. As soon as Eileithyia sets foot on Delos, Leto throws her arms around a palm tree and kneels on
    the soft meadow; the earth laughs beneath, and the child leaps out to the light. [TRADITION —
    *Hymn* 115–119]
24. Delos is laden with gold and blossoms like a mountain top in flower. [TRADITION — *Hymn*
    135–139]
25. The newborn god speaks: "The lyre and the curved bow shall ever be dear to me, and I will declare
    to men the unfailing will of Zeus." [TRADITION — *Hymn* 131–132] The script paraphrases.

**Artemis**
26. In the *Hymn*, Artemis is born on Ortygia and Apollo on Delos (line 16). In Apollodorus, Leto
    bears Artemis first, on Delos, and with Artemis acting as midwife then bears Apollo.
    [TRADITION — Apollodorus 1.4.1] The script says "in a later telling".
27. As a little girl on her father's knee, Artemis asks Zeus for lasting maidenhood, a bow and
    arrows, the mountains, and nymphs to hunt with; she will go down to the cities of men only when
    women in childbirth call her, because the Fates made her their helper from the hour she was born.
    [TRADITION — Callimachus, *Hymn* 3.6–25] Cult titles of Artemis as helper in childbirth
    (Lochia, Eileithyia) are well attested. [RECORD of cult]
28. Women who die suddenly, including in childbirth, are said to be struck by Artemis's arrows.
    [TRADITION — *Iliad* 6.428, 21.483–484] Not used; the script keeps the protective side.

**Apollo at Delphi**
29. Apollo searches for a site for his oracle, chooses the slope below Parnassus at Pytho, and kills
    with an arrow the great she-dragon that lived by the spring there. [TRADITION — *Hymn to Apollo*
    281–304, 356–374] The rotting body gives the place its name, Pytho. Cut; see OPTIONAL.
30. The Delphic oracle was consulted by cities before wars and before founding colonies for more
    than a thousand years. [RECORD — e.g. Herodotus 1.46–55, 4.150–159; the oracle ended in the late
    4th c. CE]
31. Hesiod says Zeus set the stone Cronus swallowed "fast in the wide-pathed earth at goodly Pytho
    under the glens of Parnassus, to be a sign thenceforth and a marvel to mortal men". [TRADITION —
    *Theogony* 497–500] Pausanias saw it at Delphi, oiled every day and covered with unspun wool at
    festivals. [RECORD of cult — Pausanias 10.24.6] **This is the series' physical bridge:** the
    stone from Part 1 stands in Apollo's sanctuary.

**Delos afterwards**
32. In 426/5 BCE the Athenians purified Delos: graves were removed, and it was proclaimed that no one
    should die or give birth on the island; the dying and women about to give birth were carried
    across to Rheneia. [RECORD — Thucydides 3.104; 1.8]

MOST INTERESTING FACTS
- **Zeus repeats his father's crime.** Same advisers (Earth and Sky), same fear (a son who replaces
  the king), same remedy (swallowing) — Hesiod builds the parallel himself.
- **Metis stays inside Zeus** to counsel him on good and evil: the king's wisdom is a swallowed wife.
- **The sun stopped** in the sky at Athena's birth until she took her armour off.
- **Athena holds the keys to the thunderbolt** in Aeschylus — the daughter of the wife Zeus
  swallowed is the one god he trusts with his weapon.
- **Poseidon loses again.** The brother who never accepted the draw in Part 1 loses Athens to the
  girl from Zeus's head and floods the plain.
- **The goddess of childbirth was held back by Hera** and bought with a necklace nine cubits long.
- **The earth laughed** when Apollo was born.
- **Artemis delivered her own twin** in Apollodorus, and became the goddess women call on in labour.
- **The stone Cronus swallowed stood at Delphi**, in Apollo's sanctuary.
- **No one was allowed to be born on Delos** after 426/5 BCE — the one land that let Leto give
  birth.

MYTHS / LEGENDS / TRADITIONS
The whole episode is tradition and is told as a story inside it. The narration steps outside only
three times, each attributed: "Later poets say…" (the axe), "In one Athenian tragedy…" (the keys),
"In a later telling…" (Artemis as midwife), plus Hesiod named twice (Metis inside Zeus; the stone
at Delphi). The purification of Delos is history and is told as history ("Centuries later, Athens
ruled…").

UNCERTAINTIES / DEBATES
- **Who split Zeus's head** — Hephaestus (Pindar), Prometheus or Hephaestus (Apollodorus); Hesiod
  and *Hymn* 28 have no axe at all. The script keeps the axe without a wielder.
- **Leto before or after Hera.** Hesiod lists Leto before Zeus marries Hera; the *Hymn to Apollo*
  has Hera as the jealous wife. Two traditions; the script uses the Hymn, which is the oldest full
  narrative of the birth.
- **Where Artemis was born.** Ortygia in the *Hymn*, Delos in Apollodorus. Ortygia is sometimes an
  old name for Delos itself and sometimes a separate place. The script follows Apollodorus and flags
  it as "a later telling".
- **Did Delos float.** Pindar and Callimachus, not the *Hymn*. Flagged as "in some tellings".
- **Is Athens named after Athena or the other way round.** Ancient tradition says the city took her
  name; modern linguistics tends to think the goddess was named "the Athenian one". The script never
  says which came first.
- **The judges of the contest** vary (the twelve gods, Cecrops, the citizens). The script says "the
  gods".
- **"The same prophecy"** — Cronus is told he will be overcome by his son; Zeus is told Metis' son
  will be king of gods and men. Same substance, different wording; acceptable inside the narration.

AVOIDED ON PURPOSE
- **Hephaestus** — Part 4's god. Not named as the axe-bearer; Hesiod's line that Hera, furious at
  Athena's birth, bore Hephaestus alone (*Theogony* 927–929) and the *Hymn to Apollo*'s Hera
  throwing him into the sea (316–321) are both Part 4 material.
- **Typhaon** — in the *Hymn to Apollo* (305–355) Hera, enraged by Athena's birth, bears the monster
  Typhaon alone and gives him to the she-dragon of Delphi to rear. It connects all three gods in one
  ancient poem, but it costs ~50 words and drags in Hephaestus. Kept here for the visual stage and
  for a possible Short.
- **The plot to bind Zeus** (*Iliad* 1.396–406) — Athena is one of the plotters, which is why the
  script never says she "never turned against her father".
- **Athena's virginity and the Erichthonius story**, **Arachne** (Ovid, Roman), **Medusa and the
  aegis** — strong, each its own episode; none of them is about being born.
- **Apollo's loves** (Daphne, Coronis, Hyacinthus) and **Artemis and Actaeon** — not about the
  spine.
- **Python pursuing Leto** — late (Hyginus, *Fabulae* 140). The *Hymn*'s she-dragon has no link to
  Leto, so the script does not make one.
- **"Zeus had a headache"** — modern popular framing (closest ancient source is Lucian, 2nd c. CE).
  Not used.
- **Niobe** — see OPTIONAL. Strong but dark, and it is about the twins' power, not about their birth.

STORY STRUCTURE
Beginning — the prophecy comes back: Zeus, who overthrew a swallowing father, swallows his wife.
Development — the daughter is born anyway, out of his head; what Athena became, Metis still inside
him, Athens won from Poseidon, the keys to the thunderbolt.
Escalation — Hera turns on the next mother: Leto hunted, every land refusing, a drifting rock, nine
days of labour, the goddess of childbirth held back.
Turning point — the necklace; the goddess reaches Delos; the palm, the laughing earth, Apollo.
Payoff — the twins become the goddess of birth and the god of prophecy, keeper of Cronus's stone;
Delos forbids birth; none of the three was supposed to be born; the son who would have been king
never came, and his mother is still inside Zeus.

RECOMMENDED VOICE
Ancient / mysterious — the same voice as Parts 1 and 2.

VOICE CHARACTER
Deep, atmospheric, deliberate, slightly dark. The same narrator, continuing the same account.

VOICE DELIVERY
~160 wpm, the pace Part 2 was actually delivered at (755 words in 278s). Even and cool through the
Metis opening; let "and swallowed her whole" land flat. Open up for Athena's birth — the sun
stopping is the one big image of the first half. Athena's section measured and dry. Slow down at
"Hera could not stop…": the Leto section builds steadily through the refusals and the nine days,
and breaks open at "the earth laughed beneath her". Quieter for Artemis and Delphi. The last two
sentences slow, unpushed, with a beat before "His mother is still inside Zeus."

WHY THIS VOICE FITS
Series continuity is not optional — the viewer should hear the same narrator who told the draw and
the sisters. The material also wants restraint: a swallowed wife, a hunted mother, a birth bought
with a necklace. Told darkly and evenly, the miracles (the sun stopping, the earth laughing) land
harder.
