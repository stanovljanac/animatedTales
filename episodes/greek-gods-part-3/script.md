Zeus had overthrown a father who swallowed his own children. Then he took a wife — and heard the same prophecy his father had heard.

Her name was Metis, and she was wiser than any god or mortal. Earth and Sky, the same two who had warned Cronus, told Zeus what she would bear. First a daughter, his equal in strength and judgment. Then a son, who would be king of gods and men.

Zeus did not wait for the son. He tricked Metis with soft words, and swallowed her whole.

His father had swallowed the children, and one got away. Zeus swallowed the mother. But the daughter was already inside her.

In time, Zeus gave birth to her himself — out of his own head. Later poets say it took an axe to split it open. Out leapt a grown woman in golden armour, shaking a spear. Olympus shook, the sea heaved, and the Sun stopped his horses in the sky until she took the armour off.

This was Athena.

Her mother's name meant cunning wisdom, and Athena became its goddess, in craft and in war. And Metis never left. Hesiod says Zeus kept her inside him, to counsel him on good and evil.

When the gods chose their cities, Athena and her uncle Poseidon both wanted Athens. He struck the rock of the Acropolis with his trident, and salt water welled up. She planted an olive tree. The gods gave her the city, and Poseidon, the brother who never accepted his share, flooded the plain in anger.

The daughter of the swallowed wife became her father's favourite — in one Athenian tragedy, the only god trusted with the keys to his thunderbolt.

Hera could not stop a child born from her husband's head. A child carried by another woman was different.

Leto was pregnant with Zeus's twins, and Hera hunted her across the earth. Land after land turned her away; none dared let her give birth.

At last she reached Delos — a bare rock that, in some tellings, floated loose on the sea. Even Delos was afraid the god would despise it and kick it into the waves. So Leto swore an oath: his temple would make Delos rich forever.

Her labour lasted nine days and nine nights. Every great goddess came — except Hera, who kept the goddess of childbirth beside her on Olympus. The others sent a messenger to fetch her in secret, with a bribe: a golden necklace, nine cubits long. The moment she set foot on Delos, Leto threw her arms around a palm tree, the earth laughed beneath her, and the child sprang out into the light.

It was Apollo. The whole island blossomed with gold, and the newborn god spoke at once. The lyre and the bow would be his, he said, and he would tell mankind the will of Zeus.

In a later telling, his twin sister Artemis was born first — and helped her mother deliver him. She grew up a hunter, unmarried, at home in the wild. Her mother had been turned away by every land on earth. Now every woman in labour called on her.

Apollo founded his oracle at Delphi, where he killed the serpent that lived there. For a thousand years, cities asked him what was coming before they went to war or founded colonies. And at Delphi, Hesiod says, Zeus had set up the stone his father swallowed in his place. The god of prophecy kept the stone where this whole story began.

Centuries later, Athens ruled that no one could be born on Delos, or die there. The one land that let Leto give birth became the one place in Greece where nobody could.

None of the three was supposed to be born. One came out of her father's head and ended up holding the keys to his thunderbolt. Two came off a drifting rock and became the goddess of birth and the voice of prophecy.

The only child who never came was the son who would have been king. His mother is still inside Zeus.
