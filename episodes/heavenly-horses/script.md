One of China's biggest military problems wasn't weapons. It was finding horses strong enough to use them.

In the second century BCE, the most dangerous enemy of the Han dynasty lived on horseback: the Xiongnu, nomads of the northern steppe. Emperor Wu had been fighting them for decades, and to catch riders, he needed better horses than his own.

Then word came from the far west. More than two thousand miles away, in the Ferghana Valley, lay a kingdom the Chinese called Dayuan. Its horses, people said, sweated blood, and were descended from horses of heaven.

The emperor sent an envoy with a thousand pieces of gold and a horse made of gold, to buy the best of them. Dayuan's nobles refused. According to the court historian Sima Qian, they had done the arithmetic. China's envoys lost half their men just crossing the deserts. How could it ever send an army?

The envoy lost his temper, smashed the golden horse, and left. At Dayuan's border, he was ambushed and killed.

In 104 BCE, the emperor sent an army under Li Guangli, the brother of his favourite consort. His title was also his order: General of Ershi — the city where the best horses were kept.

The oasis kingdoms along the road shut their gates and refused to feed his men. By the time the army reached Dayuan, it was a few thousand starving soldiers, and it was beaten at the same border town where the envoy had died. Two years after setting out, Li Guangli was back at the frontier with one or two men in ten.

He asked to bring them home. The emperor shut the Jade Gate, the pass into China, with an order: any soldier who tried to come through would be beheaded.

His ministers begged him to give up. He refused. If China could not beat one small kingdom, he reasoned, it would be the laughing stock of every foreign land. He had the loudest critics prosecuted, and started again.

In 102 BCE, sixty thousand soldiers marched west, with a hundred thousand oxen, more than thirty thousand horses, and donkeys and camels by the tens of thousands. The whole empire, Sima Qian wrote, was in upheaval. The army even brought two experts in judging horses, to choose the best ones when the city fell.

Thirty thousand men reached the capital of Dayuan. The city had no wells. It drew its water from a river outside the walls, so the Han brought in engineers, and moved the river.

After more than forty days, the outer wall fell. Inside, the nobles decided who was to blame: their own king, who had hidden the horses and killed the envoy. They killed him, and sent his head out with an offer. Stop the attack, and take your pick of our horses. Refuse, and we kill every good horse in the city — and an allied army is on its way.

Li Guangli took it. His experts chose several dozen of the finest horses and more than three thousand ordinary ones, and the army turned for home.

Of the sixty thousand who had marched out, just over ten thousand came back through the Jade Gate, with just over a thousand horses. Sima Qian was blunt about why so many had died. Not hunger, and not battle. Officers who stole from their own men.

A year later, Dayuan's nobles killed the king the Chinese had put on their throne. The new king sent his son to the Han court as a hostage, and agreed to send two heavenly horses east every year.

It had begun with a golden horse, offered to a kingdom that would not sell. It ended with two living horses a year.

And around the emperor's palaces, as far as the eye could see, grew fields of alfalfa from Ferghana seed: fodder, for the horses of heaven.
