# Research — The War of the Heavenly Horses (stage 03)

TOPIC
Idea #20 in `docs/ideas.md`, "The Horses That Helped Decide China's History" (69.5). Re-aimed from
the general theme — China's long dependence on steppe horses — to the one episode that has a face,
a stake and an ending: Emperor Wu's war on Dayuan, 104–101 BCE.

RECOMMENDED STORY
An emperor at war with horse nomads hears of a distant kingdom whose horses are said to come from
heaven. He offers gold; he is refused and his envoy is killed. His first army dies in the desert,
and he locks the survivors outside the empire rather than admit defeat. His second army is one of
the largest expeditions of the age. It wins — and brings home a few thousand horses, a fraction of
its men, and eventually a treaty for two horses a year.

STORY ANGLE
The idea's weakness in scoring was "no face, no stake, the thumbnail is just a horse". The war
fixes all three: a named emperor who will not back down, a general whose very title is his
destination, sixty thousand men, and a price that is written down. The angle is the arithmetic —
what was spent, what came back, and what the whole thing finally settled into.

ONE-SENTENCE HOOK (verbatim from ideas.md)
One of China's biggest military problems wasn't weapons. It was finding horses strong enough to
use them.

HISTORICAL PERIOD
Han dynasty, reign of Emperor Wu (141–87 BCE). Zhang Qian's report on Dayuan: after his return in
126 BCE. The war: first expedition 104 BCE, second 102 BCE, horses at court spring 101 BCE.

LOCATION / CULTURAL CONTEXT
Han China (capital Chang'an) → the Hexi Corridor → Dunhuang and the Jade Gate (Yumen Pass) → the
salt marsh and oasis kingdoms of the Tarim Basin → Dayuan in the Ferghana Valley (today divided
between Uzbekistan, Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan). Straight-line distance Chang'an–Ferghana is roughly
3,400 km / 2,100 miles; the Han sources put Dayuan at "about ten thousand li" (Shiji) and 12,550 li
from Chang'an (Han shu).

STORY TYPE
Historical event — Situation → Trigger → Escalation → Main event → Consequences.

KEY CHARACTERS
- **Emperor Wu of Han** — wants the horses; will not accept being refused by a small kingdom.
- **Li Guangli** — brother of the emperor's favourite, Lady Li; "General of Ershi".
- **The nobles of Dayuan** — refuse, then turn on their own king to end the siege.
- **Sima Qian** — the court historian who lived through it and wrote it down; the narration's
  source voice.
Unnamed in the script on purpose: Zhang Qian, the envoy Che Ling, King Wugua, general Jianmi,
Meicai, Chanfeng, Kangju (named only as "an allied army").

CENTRAL STORY QUESTION
How far will an empire go for a horse — and what does it get back?

CORE CONFLICT
An emperor who has decided that backing down from a small kingdom is worse than any cost, against
distance, desert and a walled city that thinks distance protects it.

TURNING POINT
The river. The Han engineers divert the stream the capital drinks from; forty days later the
nobles kill their own king and open negotiations. The war stops being about whether China can
reach Dayuan and becomes about what it will take away.

PAYOFF
Sixty thousand marched; just over ten thousand came back through the Jade Gate, with just over a
thousand horses — and Sima Qian blames not hunger or battle but officers who robbed their own men.
A year later the king China installed is murdered, and the new king agrees to send two heavenly
horses a year. It began with a golden horse offered to a kingdom that would not sell; it ended
with two living horses a year, and alfalfa planted around the palaces as far as the eye could see.

KEY EVENTS

All events are HISTORICAL RECORD unless marked. Primary sources: Sima Qian, *Shiji* 123 (Account
of Dayuan), written within a decade of the events by a contemporary; Ban Gu, *Han shu* 6 (Annals
of Emperor Wu), 22 (Treatise on Rites and Music), 61 and 96A (Western Regions).

1. Zhang Qian's report: Dayuan has many fine horses; the horses sweat blood and are said to be
   descended from heavenly horses. (*Shiji* 123: 多善馬，馬汗血，其先天馬子也.) The "heavenly"
   descent is TRADITION as reported, not a claim the script endorses.
2. An earlier divination from the *Book of Changes* had told the emperor that divine horses would
   come from the northwest. He first gave the name "heavenly horses" to horses from the Wusun; when
   he obtained Dayuan's, he renamed the Wusun horses "Western Extremity" and gave "heavenly horses"
   to Dayuan's. (*Shiji* 123.) — cut from the script for time.
3. The envoy Che Ling is sent with a thousand pieces of gold and a golden horse to buy the fine
   horses kept in the city of Ershi. (使壯士車令等持千金及金馬以請宛王貳師城善馬.)
4. Dayuan's nobles refuse, reasoning that Han is too far away: envoys travel in parties of hundreds,
   run short of food, and more than half die — "how could it send a great army?"
   (漢使數百人為輩來，而常乏食，死者過半，是安能致大軍乎.) The script attributes this reasoning to
   Sima Qian by name.
5. The envoy curses them, smashes the golden horse and leaves; the nobles have the eastern border
   town of Yucheng intercept and kill him and take the goods. (椎金馬而去 … 令其東邊郁成遮攻殺漢使.)
6. 104 BCE: Li Guangli, brother of Lady Li, appointed "Ershi General" — the title named after the
   city he was to reach to take the horses. The emperor's stated purpose in choosing him was to
   make Lady Li's family eligible for a marquisate. (欲侯寵姬李氏 … 期至貳師城取善馬，故號貳師將軍.)
7. First army: 6,000 cavalry from dependent peoples and "several tens of thousands" of young
   delinquents from the commanderies (屬國六千騎，及郡國惡少年數萬人). *Wikipedia's* "20,000
   infantry" is not in *Shiji*; the script uses the primary wording, "tens of thousands".
8. The oasis states shut their gates and refuse food; by Yucheng only a few thousand remain, starving
   and exhausted, and they are badly beaten there. (當道小國恐，各堅城守，不肯給食 … 士至者不過數千，
   皆飢罷.) Note: the army had attacked oasis towns along the way, so Yucheng is **not** "the first
   city it attacked" — the script says "the same border town where the envoy had died", which is
   exact.
9. Round trip two years; back at Dunhuang with "no more than one or two in ten" (往來二歲 …
   士不過什一二). The emperor sends officers to block the Jade Gate: any soldier who dares enter will
   be beheaded (使使遮玉門，曰軍有敢入者輒斬之). Li Guangli stays at Dunhuang.
10. That summer a separate Han army of 20,000+ under Zhao Ponu is lost to the Xiongnu
    (其夏，漢亡浞野之兵二萬餘於匈奴). Ministers want to abandon Dayuan and concentrate on the
    Xiongnu. The emperor's reasoning: if small Dayuan cannot be taken, Bactria and others will
    despise Han, the fine horses will never come, and Han will be the laughing stock of foreign
    states (宛小國而不能下 … 為外國笑). He prosecutes Deng Guang and the others who spoke most
    strongly against it (案言伐宛尤不便者鄧光等). The Zhao Ponu loss is cut from the script.
11. 102 BCE: 60,000 men leave Dunhuang, not counting private followers; 100,000 oxen, 30,000+
    horses, donkeys, mules and camels by the tens of thousands; convicts pardoned to serve; 180,000
    extra garrison troops north of Jiuquan and Zhangye; "the whole empire in upheaval"
    (天下騷動). Two men skilled with horses are appointed colonels to choose the fine horses when
    Dayuan falls (拜習馬者二人為執驅校尉，備破宛擇取其善馬云).
12. This time the oasis states come out and supply the army; Luntou refuses, is besieged for some
    days and massacred (屠之). Cut from the script for time.
13. 30,000 reach Dayuan (漢兵到者三萬人). Dayuan's troops come out, are shot down, and withdraw
    behind the walls.
14. The capital has no wells and draws water from a stream outside; Han water engineers divert it
    (宛王城中無井，皆汲城外流水，於是乃遣水工徙其城下水).
15. Forty-odd days of siege; the outer wall falls; the brave general Jianmi is captured.
16. The nobles blame King Wugua for hiding the horses and killing the envoys, kill him and send his
    head with terms: stop attacking and take the fine horses as you please, and we will feed your
    army; refuse and we kill all the fine horses — and Kangju's relief army is coming.
    (我盡出善馬，恣所取 … 即不聽，我盡殺善馬，而康居之救且至.)
17. Li Guangli learns the city has recently acquired "Qin men" who know how to dig wells, and still
    has plenty of food (聞宛城中新得秦人，知穿井，而其內食尚多). He accepts. — Kept in research,
    cut from the script; the threat alone carries the decision.
18. Han takes several dozen fine horses and 3,000+ stallions and mares of middling and lower
    quality (善馬數十匹，中馬以下牡牝三千餘匹); installs Meicai, a noble who had treated Han envoys
    well; withdraws without entering the inner city.
19. Return: just over 10,000 men and just over 1,000 army horses enter the Jade Gate
    (軍入玉門者萬餘人，軍馬千餘匹). Sima Qian: the army was not short of food and battle deaths
    were not many, but the officers were greedy, did not care for the men and preyed on them, and
    that is why so many died (軍非乏食，戰死不能多，而將吏貪，多不愛士卒，侵牟之，以此物故眾).
20. Spring 101 BCE: the head of Dayuan's king and the horses reach court; a "Song of the Heavenly
    Horses of the Western Extremity" is composed (*Han shu* 6: 作西極天馬之歌). Li Guangli is made
    Marquis of Haixi.
21. More than a year later, Dayuan's nobles kill Meicai for "flattering Han and bringing slaughter on
    the country", enthrone Wugua's brother Chanfeng, who sends a son as hostage to the Han court and
    agrees to send two heavenly horses a year (*Han shu* 96A: 歲獻天馬二匹).
22. Alfalfa and grapes: Han envoys brought back the seeds; when heavenly horses became many and
    foreign envoys numerous, alfalfa and grapes were planted beside the detached palaces as far as
    the eye could see (*Shiji* 123: 離宮別觀旁盡種蒲萄、苜蓿極望). The horses were fond of alfalfa
    (馬嗜苜蓿).

CAUSES
The Xiongnu wars and the Han need for better cavalry mounts; the emperor's desire for Dayuan's
horses specifically; the refusal and the killing of the envoy; the emperor's judgement that losing
to a small state would cost Han its standing across the Western Regions.

CONSEQUENCES
Dayuan submitted and sent hostages and annual horses; the Western Regions states sent envoys and
hostages; Han posted stations from Dunhuang westward toward the salt marsh and set up agricultural
garrisons at Luntou to supply its envoys (*Shiji* 123). Li Guangli went on to command against the
Xiongnu, surrendered to them in 90 BCE and was killed there — outside this story.

MOST INTERESTING FACTS
- **The general's title was his destination.** "Ershi General" — named for the city of the horses.
- **Dayuan's refusal was a logistics argument**, recorded almost verbatim: Han's envoys already lose
  half their men on the road, so an army is impossible. They were wrong by sixty thousand men.
- **The emperor locked his own army out of the empire** at the Jade Gate rather than let it come
  home defeated.
- **The army packed horse judges** — two officers appointed in advance to choose the horses.
- **The siege was won by moving a river**, and ended by the besieged killing their own king.
- **Sima Qian blames the officers, not the enemy**, for the losses on the return.
- **Two horses a year.** After all of it, the lasting arrangement was an annual gift of two.
- **Alfalfa to the horizon** around the palaces — the plants came back with the horses.

MYTHS / LEGENDS / TRADITIONS
- "Blood-sweating" and "descended from heavenly horses" are the Han report, not established fact.
  The script says "people said". Modern explanations (not in the script): a parasitic nematode,
  *Parafilaria multipapillosa*, which causes bleeding skin nodules in horses; or the sheen of sweat
  on a reddish coat. INTERPRETATION.
- The *Book of Changes* divination that promised divine horses from the northwest — recorded in
  *Shiji*, cut for time.

UNCERTAINTIES / DEBATES
- **What the emperor wanted the horses for.** Arthur Waley (*The Heavenly Horses of Ferghana*, 1955)
  argued the motive was religious: the hymn has the heavenly horse "open the far gate" and carry the
  singer to Kunlun, the mountain of the immortals, and to the gates of heaven — and Emperor Wu is
  well documented as seeking immortality. H. G. Creel ("The Role of the Horse in Chinese History", 1965) answered that the sources show the
  horses used as cavalry and that the military need was real. The script states the military motive
  in its setup (the Xiongnu) and does **not** enter the debate — it was cut for time, and the
  hymn with it. If the episode runs short, the hymn and the debate are the first thing to restore
  (~55 words, drafted in the note below).
- **The 1,000 horses at the Jade Gate** are "army horses" (軍馬). The text does not say how many of
  the 3,000 Ferghana horses were among them. The script says "with just over a thousand horses" and
  does not claim that two thousand of the Ferghana horses died.
- **Location of the Jade Gate in 104 BCE.** Li Guangli "stays at Dunhuang" while barred from the
  Jade Gate, which fits the view that the pass then lay east of Dunhuang and was moved west later.
  The script says "the frontier" and never places Dunhuang relative to the gate.
- **Who Dayuan were.** Some writers connect Dayuan with Greek settlers in Ferghana after Alexander
  (the "Yavana" etymology; Alexandria Eschate near Khujand). Contested; left out entirely.

AVOIDED ON PURPOSE
- Zhang Qian's thirteen-year journey — a full episode of its own; here he is "word from the far west".
- Personal names beyond Emperor Wu, Li Guangli and Sima Qian.
- The massacre at Luntou and the king's severed head as images; the narration states them once,
  plainly.

ESSENTIAL MATERIAL
The Xiongnu problem and why horses mattered; the report of Dayuan's horses; the golden horse offer,
the refusal and its reasoning, the smashed horse and the murdered envoy; Li Guangli and the Ershi
title; the first army's collapse and the Jade Gate order; the emperor's refusal to stop and his
reason; the second mobilisation in numbers; the diverted river; the forty-day siege; the nobles
killing their king and the offer; the horses chosen; the return numbers and Sima Qian's verdict;
the two-horses-a-year settlement; alfalfa around the palaces.

OPTIONAL MATERIAL (cut first if long, restore first if short)
- Hymn and motive debate (~55 words): *"The emperor had a hymn written for his heavenly horses. In
  it, they come across the moving sands from the western edge of the world — and carry him up to
  the gates of heaven. Historians still argue whether he wanted cavalry, or a way to heaven."*
- The Zhao Ponu army of 20,000 lost that same summer (why the ministers wanted to stop).
- Luntou refusing and being massacred.
- The Chinese well-diggers inside the city (why Li Guangli accepted).
- Blood-sweating explained.

STORY STRUCTURE
Beginning — the hook: the problem was never weapons, it was horses; the Xiongnu and the emperor.
Development — the report of Dayuan; the golden horse; the refusal and why; the murdered envoy.
Escalation — the first army dies in the desert; the Jade Gate is shut on it; the emperor doubles
down and mobilises an empire.
Turning point — the river is moved; forty days; the nobles kill their king and offer the horses.
Payoff — the numbers at the Jade Gate and why; two horses a year; alfalfa to the horizon.

RECOMMENDED VOICE
Historian / documentary.

VOICE CHARACTER
Mature, calm, articulate, authoritative. A narrator who has read the ledger and reports it without
raising the voice.

VOICE DELIVERY
~145 wpm. Clear and even through the setup; the Dayuan nobles' reasoning lightly, with their
confidence audible. Slow for the Jade Gate order, pause after. The second mobilisation is a list —
read it as one. Flat and quiet for the return numbers; the irony of the ending is carried by the
words, not by emphasis.

WHY THIS VOICE FITS
The drama is in the arithmetic, and the arithmetic is on the record. A Commander voice would turn
the siege into the climax; the story's real climax is the return count and the two horses a year,
and those land only in a calm, documentary register. Dark / dramatic would over-sell the losses.
