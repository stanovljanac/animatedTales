# Research — A King Gave Away So Much Gold That Cairo's Price Fell (stage 03)

TOPIC
New idea #35 in `docs/ideas.md` (added 2026-11-04, outside the original thirty): Mansa Musa of Mali in
Cairo in 1324, told through al-Umari's account of what an Egyptian emir saw, and then through what
survives of it. First African story on the channel (fills the Africa region) and the first set in the
Middle Ages (the site's Middle Ages era was empty). Popularity is **not measured**: the search tool
returns no view counts. Mansa Musa is a very commonly told subject; the angle here is the source itself.

RECOMMENDED STORY
In July 1324 Mansa Musa, ruler of Mali, reached Cairo on his pilgrimage to Mecca, camped three days by
the pyramids and stayed three months. An Egyptian emir sent to meet him later told the historian
al-Umari what he saw: Musa spoke only through an interpreter although his Arabic was perfect, sent
many loads of raw gold to the sultan's treasury, and refused the sultan's audience ("I came for the
Pilgrimage and nothing else") because he would have to kiss the ground; when told to kiss the ground
he said "How may this be?", a man beside him whispered, and he said "I make obeisance to God who
created me" and bowed. He then "flooded Cairo with his benefactions", leaving no emir or office-holder
without a load of gold, and Cairo's people made huge profits and "depressed its value" in gold.
Al-Umari adds numbers: a mithqal had not gone below 25 dirhams; afterwards it did not exceed 22, "for
about twelve years until this day". The script then says plainly that the twelve years rest on
al-Umari alone, that al-Maqrizi gives a different figure and that Warren Schultz found the changes
within normal fluctuation. The return from Mecca ruined Musa: many pilgrims died, supplies were lost,
and he borrowed from Cairo's merchants (lenders unpaid or partly paid per al-Umari and Ibn Khaldun;
other sources disagree). About 1375 the Catalan Atlas shows him with gold and calls him "the richest
and noblest of all these lands" — a caption modern writers turned into "richest man in history".

STORY ANGLE
Not "richest man ever". A man who bowed only to God and gave gold away like water, and a gold price
that may not have moved at all, set against the one thing that clearly survived: the Cairenes' eagerness
to tell it (al-Umari's own first lines).

ONE-SENTENCE HOOK (verbatim into ideas.md and the script)
In 1324 a king from West Africa rode into Cairo and gave away so much gold that, one chronicler says,
the price of gold stayed low for twelve years.

HISTORICAL PERIOD
Pilgrimage 1324–1325 (Wikipedia gives 1324–1325 on one page and 1324–1326 on another). Reign c. 1312 to
c. 1337. Al-Umari wrote around 1337 (Wikipedia) — the Cairo-visit date of al-Umari is described
differently on the same page ("shortly after the pilgrimage"); the script therefore says "later" and
never "years later". Catalan Atlas c. 1375–1380s. For the site: **Middle Ages (1324)**.

LOCATION / CULTURAL CONTEXT
Mali (West African Sahel), the road across the Sahara, Mamluk Cairo under the sultan al-Nasir
Muhammad, the pyramids at Giza, Mecca. Majorca (the Catalan Atlas).

STORY TYPE
Historical event with a mystery tail: Situation → Trigger (the audience) → Escalation (the giving) →
Main event (the price) → What we know and do not → Consequences (debt; the map; the label).

KEY CHARACTERS
Mansa Musa; the sultan al-Nasir; the unnamed emir (the text prints "Abu…"; Wikipedia says Ibn Amir
Hajib, via a secondary page); al-Umari; al-Maqrizi, Ibn Khaldun and Warren Schultz as voices on the
numbers; Cresques Abraham (the Atlas), optional.

CENTRAL STORY QUESTION
Did one king's generosity really change the price of gold in Cairo, and what did it leave behind?

CORE CONFLICT
A king who will not kiss the ground, and then buys goodwill with gold, against a record that may
overstate what the gold did.

TURNING POINT
"Whether it is true is another matter."

PAYOFF
Nobody can say what happened to the price; what is certain is that al-Umari found the people of Cairo
eager to tell him what they had seen.

KEY EVENTS (E essential, S supporting, O optional)
1. **S** July 1324: arrival; three days at the pyramids; Nile crossed on 19 July; three months in Cairo,
   leaving 18 October (Wikipedia, *Mansa Musa*; secondary).
2. **E** The emir's account (al-Umari, translated by Levtzion and Hopkins 1981, pp. 269–273): interpreter;
   loads of "unworked native gold" to the treasury; the refusal; the whisper; "I make obeisance to God
   who created me"; the sultan "half rose to greet him and sat him by his side"; robes of honour.
3. **E** "He flooded Cairo with his benefactions. He left no court emir nor holder of a royal office
   without the gift of a load of gold. The Cairenes made incalculable profits out of him and his suite in
   buying and selling and giving and taking. They exchanged gold until they depressed its value in Egypt
   and caused its price to fall."
4. **E** Al-Umari on the price: "The mithqal did not go below 25 dirhams and was generally above, but from
   that time its value fell … The mithqal does not exceed 22 dirhams or less. This has been the state of
   affairs for about twelve years until this day".
5. **E** The doubt: Schultz — changes "well within normal fluctuations in the value of gold in Mamluk
   Egypt" (Wikipedia, quoting); al-Maqrizi — "the rate of the gold dinar fell by six dirhams" (Wikipedia,
   *Mali Empire*); the twelve years only in al-Umari.
6. **E** The return: by Suez "many of the Malian pilgrims had died of cold, starvation, or bandit raids, and
   they had lost much of their supplies"; Musa "forced to borrow money and resell much of what they had
   purchased while in Cairo", in debt to merchants such as Siraj al-Din; al-Umari and Ibn Khaldun: lenders
   "never repaid or only partly repaid"; "other sources disagree" (Wikipedia).
7. **E** The Catalan Atlas: caption quoted by Wikipedia — "This black Lord is called Musse Melly … This king
   is the richest and noblest of all these lands due to the abundance of gold that is extracted from his
   lands." Caption of the image: "holding a gold object" (atlas page) vs "a gold coin" (Mali page); the
   script says "with gold in his hand". Wikipedia reports Hadrien Collet on the later "fabrication of the
   Mansa Musa wealth myth".
8. **E** Al-Umari's opening lines: "From the beginning of my coming to stay in Egypt I heard talk of the
   arrival of this sultan Musa on his Pilgrimage and found the Cairenes eager to recount what they had
   seen of the Africans' prodigal spending."

CAUSES
Mali's control of gold and salt trade; a pilgrimage that doubled as a diplomatic event.

CONSEQUENCES
Musa's debts; Mali appears on European maps (Dulcert 1339, Catalan Atlas); the label that outgrew its source.

MOST INTERESTING FACTS
- The refusal and the whisper at the sultan's court (al-Umari's own report).
- The emir saw that Musa's Arabic was perfect and he still spoke through an interpreter.
- A mithqal is about 4.5 g of gold (Wikipedia, *Mali Empire*).
- The atlas caption says richest of "these lands", not of the world.

MYTHS / LEGENDS / TRADITIONS
Mostly HR (a contemporary-era report), with INTERPRETATION on the price. The "richest man ever" label is a
modern legend. Not used: entourage numbers (12,000 slaves; 80 camels with 50–300 lb of gold each) and
"18 tons" of gold — Wikipedia calls them "rhetorical rather than statistical", from a single chain of
transmission (Schultz 2006; Collet 2019).

UNCERTAINTIES / DEBATES
1. The twelve-year gold price: al-Umari alone; Schultz disputes; al-Maqrizi gives a different figure.
2. The emir's identity: the translation prints "Abu…"; Ibn Amir Hajib comes from Wikipedia and a popular page.
3. Years of the pilgrimage: 1324–1325 or –1326; al-Umari's dating (c. 1337 vs "shortly after").
4. Debts: repayment disputed; one footnote has Musa leaving Cairo for Mali "a year later".
5. Timbuktu: annexed in 1324 or on the return (Tarikh al-Sudan); the architect al-Sahili's role is
   "a historiographical myth" per the page. **Not used.**
6. Catalan Atlas: "gold object" or "gold coin"; the caption's "Gineva" is glossed (Ghana) by Wikipedia.
7. Mali's gold sources: the pages disagree on whether the Mali Empire controlled gold-producing regions
   directly. The script says only that the kingdom "taxed the trade in gold and salt" and that West Africa
   "supplied much of the gold" (Wikipedia: roughly two thirds of the gold in the medieval Mediterranean).

RECOMMENDED VOICE
Historian / documentary.

VOICE CHARACTER
Calm, warm, a little dry; the voice of someone reading a source to you and then asking if it is true.

VOICE DELIVERY
~143 wpm. Quiet on the quotations at court; brisk on the list of profits; a beat before "Whether it is true
is another matter."; unhurried ending.

WHY THIS VOICE FITS
A documentary voice suits a story built on one text and its doubts; an epic voice would turn a pilgrim into a
conqueror.
