# Mansa Musa in Cairo: The Gold, the Bow and the Question of What Really Happened

[![Mansa Musa, the king of Mali, in the 2D animated story of his visit to Cairo]({{THUMBNAIL}})]({{VIDEO_URL}})

Some years after a great visitor had left, a scholar arrived in Cairo and found that people still wanted to talk about him. Al-Umari heard it again and again: the Africans had spent without restraint, and everyone seemed to have a memory of it. The man at the centre of those memories was Mansa Musa, ruler of Mali, who had passed through the city in 1324 on his way to Mecca.

What al-Umari wrote down is the closest thing we have to standing in the crowd. It is also a source that has to be read with care.

## Who came to Cairo

Musa ruled Mali from around 1312 to around 1337. His state, in the West African Sahel, drew revenue from the commerce in salt and gold, and West African gold made up a large share of what circulated around the medieval Mediterranean. A pilgrimage to Mecca was a religious duty, and for a ruler on this scale it was also a diplomatic event.

He reached Egypt in July 1324. His party made camp near the pyramids of Giza for three days, crossed the Nile, and remained in the city for about three months. Egypt was then ruled by the Mamluk sultan al-Nasir Muhammad, who sent one of his emirs out to welcome the guest. That officer's report, passed on to al-Umari, is the heart of the story. The translation prints his name only in part, so it is safest to call him the emir.

## The king who would not kneel

The emir noticed something shrewd. Musa could speak Arabic perfectly well, yet he answered only through an interpreter. He was generous from the start, sending quantities of unworked gold to the royal treasury. A visit in person was another matter. Court custom required a guest to put his lips to the floor before the sultan, and the king of Mali had no wish to do it. He had travelled for the pilgrimage, he explained, and had no other business.

The emir pressed him until he gave way. When the moment came and the instruction was given, Musa asked how such a thing could be. A man near him murmured in his ear. Musa then declared that he was paying homage to God, his creator, and lowered himself to the ground. The gesture the court wanted had been performed, but its meaning had moved elsewhere. The sultan rose partway to greet him, seated him beside himself, and robes of honour followed.

## Gold in every hand

Then came the part Cairo remembered. According to the emir, no officer of the court and no holder of royal office was left without a load of gold. Traders did extraordinarily well out of the visitors, who bought, sold and gave freely. So much metal changed hands, he said, that gold lost value in Egypt.

Al-Umari put figures to it. The measure was the mithqal, a weight of gold of about four and a half grams, priced in silver dirhams. Before the visit it had not dropped below twenty-five dirhams. Afterwards it did not climb past twenty-two, and al-Umari wrote that this had lasted some twelve years, up to the time he was writing.

That claim travelled a long way. It rests, however, on one author. Al-Maqrizi, a later chronicler, gives a different measure of the damage, saying the gold dinar fell by six dirhams. The modern historian Warren Schultz compared recorded exchange rates and concluded that the movements sat well within the ordinary fluctuations of Mamluk Egypt. None of this means Musa did not spend lavishly. It means that lavish spending and a ruined market are two separate claims, and only the first is easy to believe.

The enormous numbers sometimes attached to the caravan, with thousands of attendants and tonnes of metal, deserve the same caution. Scholars have described such figures as rhetorical rather than statistical.

## The long way home

The return from Mecca was grim. By the time the travellers reached Suez, cold, hunger and bandit attacks had killed many of them, and much of what they carried was gone. The king who had handed out gold now needed credit. He borrowed from merchants in Cairo and sold back goods he had bought in the city. Al-Umari and Ibn Khaldun report that the lenders were never repaid, or were repaid only in part. Other sources tell it differently.

## A figure on a map

Around 1375, about half a century after the visit, the Catalan Atlas, made on Majorca, showed a crowned African ruler holding gold. Its caption praises him as the richest and noblest king of those lands, because of the abundance of gold found in his country.

Notice the limit in that sentence. It compares him with the rulers of a region. Modern retellings often stretch it into the richest human being of all time, a title the caption never gave him. The historian Hadrien Collet has written about how the legend of his wealth was later built up.
