In 1324 a king from West Africa rode into Cairo and gave away so much gold that, one chronicler says, the price of gold stayed low for twelve years.

He was Mansa Musa, the ruler of Mali, on his way to Mecca. His kingdom taxed the trade in gold and salt, and West Africa supplied much of the gold that reached the Mediterranean. He arrived in July, camped for three days beside the pyramids, and stayed in Cairo three months.

The sultan, al-Nasir, sent an emir to meet him. That emir later told what he saw to a historian named al-Umari.

Musa spoke to him only through an interpreter, though his Arabic was perfect. He sent many loads of raw gold to the sultan's treasury. But when the emir urged him to visit the sultan himself, he refused. "I came for the pilgrimage and nothing else," he said. The emir understood. At court, Musa would have to kiss the ground.

The emir kept pressing, and at last Musa agreed. In the sultan's presence they told him: kiss the ground. "How may this be?" said Musa. A man beside him whispered something. "I make obeisance to God who created me," he said, and bowed. The sultan half rose and sat him by his own side.

Then he began to give. "He flooded Cairo with his benefactions," the emir said. "He left no court emir, nor holder of a royal office, without the gift of a load of gold." The people of Cairo bought and sold with Musa and his men, and made incalculable profits. They exchanged gold, the emir said, until its value in Egypt fell.

Al-Umari had numbers. Before Musa, a mithqal of gold, a standard weight, was never worth less than twenty-five silver dirhams. Afterward, never more than twenty-two. That had been so, he wrote, "for about twelve years until this day."

Whether it is true is another matter. The twelve years come from al-Umari alone. Another chronicler, al-Maqrizi, says the gold dinar lost six dirhams. And the modern historian Warren Schultz, comparing recorded exchange rates, found changes well within the normal swings of Mamluk Egypt.

What the gold did cost was Musa himself. On the road back from Mecca, many of his pilgrims died of cold, hunger and bandits, and much of his baggage was lost. With his money gone, he borrowed from Cairo's merchants and sold what he had bought there. Al-Umari and Ibn Khaldun both say the lenders were never repaid, or only in part, though other sources disagree.

The story kept traveling. Around 1375, a mapmaker on Majorca drew him in the Catalan Atlas, with gold in his hand. The caption calls him "the richest and noblest of all these lands." Today he is called the richest man who ever lived. The mapmaker said something smaller: richest of those lands.

So nobody can say for certain what happened to the price of gold. But al-Umari tells us how his story began. From the day he came to live in Egypt, he writes, he heard talk of the arrival of the sultan Musa, and found the people of Cairo eager to tell him what they had seen.
