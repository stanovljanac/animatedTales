The lords of the Maya underworld invited two brothers to a ball game. It was a trap, and the brothers knew it. It was how their father had died.

The story is told in the Popol Vuh, the book of the K'iche' Maya of Guatemala. It was written down in the 1550s, after the Spanish conquest, and copied by a priest around 1701.

The father, Hun Hunahpu, and his brother played ball so loudly that the lords of Xibalba, the underworld, heard it overhead. They sent owls with an invitation. The brothers crossed the rivers of blood and went down. The lords had set wooden figures in the seats of honor, and the brothers greeted them. Then the lords laughed. They offered the brothers a bench to sit on. It was a burning stone. They locked them in the House of Darkness with a torch and a cigar each, and demanded them back unburned. By morning, both had burned down. The brothers were sacrificed, and buried in the ballcourt. Hun Hunahpu's head was hung in a tree, and the tree began to bear fruit.

A young woman came to look. The head spat into her hand, and she conceived twins: Hunahpu and Xbalanque.

They grew up playing ball, and one day they too made the underworld shake. The same lords sent the same message: come, and bring your ball. It reached them through a louse, swallowed by a toad, swallowed by a snake, swallowed by a hawk. Before they left, the twins planted two canes in the middle of their grandmother's house. If the canes dried, they were dead. If they sprouted, they lived.

They crossed the rivers on their blowguns. A mosquito went ahead and bit each lord in turn. Each cried out, and the next named him. So the twins greeted every lord by name, and refused the burning bench.

Then came the nights. In the House of Darkness, they lit the lords' torch with a macaw's red feathers and their cigars with fireflies, and returned them unburned. In the House of Cold they did not freeze, and the jaguars were thrown bones. In the House of Bats, Hunahpu looked out to see if dawn had come, and a bat cut off his head.

His brother called every animal in the world. The turtle took the place of the head. In the next ball game, the lords used the real head as their ball, and Xbalanque set a rabbit loose. While the lords chased it, he swapped the heads back.

But the twins knew their end. They told two soothsayers how to answer when the lords asked what to do with their bones: grind them like maize, and scatter them in the river. The lords built an oven. The twins embraced and threw themselves in.

The lords did as they were told. But the bones sank to the bottom of the river, and on the fifth day two fish-men rose. Then two ragged wanderers appeared and danced. They burned a house and made it whole. They sacrificed each other, and rose.

The lords were delighted. "Do the same to us," they said. The twins killed the two chief lords, One Death and Seven Death, and did not bring them back.

Then the twins honored their father, and rose into the sky. One became the sun, the other the moon.

No one knows how old the story is. Maya vases from a thousand years before the book show figures that may be the twins, but the book itself was written after the conquest, and scholars still argue about what it kept.

Their father lost because he did not know the tricks. The twins won because they had planned their own death. And the lords of death, it turns out, could not resist a good show.
