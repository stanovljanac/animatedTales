# The Maya Hero Twins of the Popol Vuh: How Hunahpu and Xbalanque Outwitted the Lords of Death

[![Hunahpu and Xbalanque, the Maya hero twins, in the 2D animated story of their journey into the underworld]({{THUMBNAIL}})]({{VIDEO_URL}})

In the highlands of Guatemala, the story goes, two brothers played ball with such enthusiasm that the noise carried down into the underworld. The lords who ruled there did not enjoy being disturbed overhead, and they sent four owls to fetch the players. The brothers went. They never came back.

That is only the first half of the Hero Twins' story, and it ends badly. The brothers who died were Hun Hunahpu and Vucub Hunahpu. The twins, Hunahpu and Xbalanque, are the sons of the first of them, and everything they do afterwards is an answer to how their father was killed.

## A book written after the conquest

The Popol Vuh is the sacred book of the K'iche' Maya. It was set down in K'iche' around 1550 to 1558, in the years after the Spanish conquest, from a tradition that had until then lived in memory. Nobody knows who the writer was. The copy we can read comes from a Dominican friar, Francisco Ximénez, who transcribed it around 1701 at Chichicastenango and added a Spanish translation. A small minority of scholars even doubts that a written text existed before him.

Most readers still treat the twins as much older than the book. Figures in Classic Maya art, made roughly between 200 and 900 AD, have been identified as the twins, and the murals at San Bartolo, older again, may show Hunahpu. "May" is the honest word. What the book kept and what it changed is still argued.

## Xibalba, the place of fright

Xibalba means "place of fright", and its rulers were a council. At the head sat One Death and Seven Death. Below them came ten more lords in pairs, with names such as Flying Scab, Gathered Blood, Pus Demon, Bone Staff and Packstrap. They are vain and curious more than monstrous, and they cheat.

The ballgame is the thread through the whole story. In the Popol Vuh it is tied to death and to overcoming it. It is tempting to read every Mesoamerican ball game as a sacrifice, but the archaeology is more careful: the link appears rather late in the record, and even the images are hedged. The book tells a story about a game, not a rulebook for one.

## The father's trap

The summons to Hun Hunahpu and his brother came with a question from the lords: who are these who begin to play over our heads? The brothers left their ball in a hole in the roof, said goodbye, crossed ravines and rivers, and reached a crossroads of four roads. In the council hall the lords had seated two wooden figures in the places of honour. The brothers greeted the figures as lords, and the real lords laughed.

Invited to sit, they sat on a bench of burning stone. They were shut into the House of Darkness with a pine torch and a cigar apiece, to be handed back whole by morning. By morning both had burned away, and the lords had what they wanted. The brothers were sacrificed and buried in the ballcourt, and Hun Hunahpu's head was set in a tree, which promptly bore fruit like calabashes.

A daughter of one of the lords came to look. The head spat into her palm. "In that saliva I have given you my posterity," it says in the Spanish translation. She conceived, and the twins were born.

## Two canes and a mosquito

Years later the twins, grown into ballplayers, made the same noise, and the same lords sent the same invitation. This time it travelled through their grandmother's louse, swallowed by a toad, the toad by a snake, the snake by a hawk, until the hawk was shot and the louse could speak. Before leaving, the twins planted two canes in the middle of the house. Canes that dried would mean their death, canes that budded that they lived.

The rivers they crossed on their blowguns. At the crossroads they sent a mosquito ahead to bite each lord in turn, and every lord cried out, so that the next one named him. The twins arrived knowing every name, greeted each lord correctly, and would not sit on the bench. ## The houses

In the retellings behind this article the nights come as six houses: Darkness, a house of blades (rendered Obsidian, Lances or Razor depending on the translation), Cold, Jaguars, Fire and Bats. The video keeps to a few of them, but the pattern is the same everywhere. Each house is a trick, and each trick is answered by a small piece of cleverness: a macaw's red tail feather standing in for the burning torch, fireflies for the lit cigar, a gift of bones for the hungry jaguars.

In the House of Bats the twins slept inside their blowguns. Hunahpu put his head out to see whether it was dawn, and the Death Bat took it off. Xbalanque summoned the animals, a turtle served as a head, and in the lords' next ball game a loose rabbit drew the chase away while the real head was put back.

## Dying on their own terms

The turning point is a single line: the twins knew their end. They told two soothsayers what to answer when the lords asked what to do with their bones. The lords built a hot oven of stone. "Do not mock us like this," the twins say in the Spanish text, "do we not know that we shall die?" They embraced and stepped into it.

The bones were ground and thrown into the river, exactly as advised, and the river did the rest. On the fifth day two fish-men rose, and next came two ragged wanderers who danced the dances of the owl, the weasel, the armadillo and the centipede. They burned a house and made it whole. They killed a dog and brought it back. They sacrificed one another and rose again.

The lords asked for the same treatment. The twins killed One Death first, then seized Seven Death and did not revive him. The rest fled, then surrendered, and the twins took away the power of the lords of Xibalba over living men. Then the canes sprouted, the grandmother burned copal for them, and the twins honoured their father and rose into the sky, one as the sun and the other as the moon.

## What the sources leave open

Even the cast changes with the translation. A Spanish version made in 1927 from a French one renders the names by meaning, so Hunahpu becomes "Maestro Mago" and Xbalanque "Brujito". The houses vary. Spence's 1908 retelling has the father and uncle ascending as sun and moon, where the Spanish text gives it to the twins. Whether the father's body is retrieved or left buried in the ballcourt depends on whom you read.

What stays constant is the reversal. The father lost a game that had been rigged against him. His sons won because they understood the rigging, and then planned their own deaths around it. The lords of Xibalba were undone by wanting to see the trick one more time, this time on themselves.
