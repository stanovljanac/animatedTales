How do you move a herd of animals that weigh ten times what you do, run much faster than you, and have horns, when you have no horse and no rifle? On the plains of what is now Alberta, hunters solved it for nearly six thousand years. They did not chase the herd. They persuaded it.

Nobody alive has seen a hunt at Head-Smashed-In. What we know comes from archaeology, from travelers who watched similar hunts in the nineteenth century, and from what Blackfoot elders have told the archaeologists who worked there.

A sandstone cliff rises about ten metres above the foothills of the Rocky Mountains. Behind it, a wide basin of grassland stretches away. Months before a hunt, people burned it, so that fresh grass would draw the bison. And leading toward the cliff, they built lanes of stone cairns, hundreds of them, more than ten kilometres of them, widening like a funnel. Archaeologists think brush and sod were stacked on the stones to make them taller.

Everything depended on the wind. A bison's best defense is its nose, and it can smell a person several kilometres away. So the wind had to blow from the herd, over the funnel, and never the other way.

The hunt began with ceremony, and with young men called buffalo runners. They put on wolf hides, or the skins of bison calves, and rubbed themselves with sage and fat to hide their scent. Then they crept toward the herd, staying downwind. They did not move like men. They moved like animals. Some of them cried out like a calf that has lost its mother, and a cow cannot ignore that sound. The cows raised their heads and followed the lost calves, one slow step at a time, toward the wide end of the lanes. Others had other tricks. One traveler wrote that hunters crawled toward a herd in single file, and that the bison, the hunters said, took the line for a big snake.

Behind the cairns, people waited, hidden. Men, women and children. When the herd had passed, they stood up behind it, closing the way back, and along the lanes they waved robes to turn aside any animal that strayed. It was not a stampede. It was patience, in short bursts and long pauses, because the herd had to reach the cliff as one tight pack. The archaeologist Jack Brink describes a theory: if a straggling line of two hundred bison reaches the edge, you kill none of them. If a tight pack of two hundred does, you kill them all.

Only at the very end did the hunters let the herd run: downhill, around a curve that hid the cliff. The leaders saw the edge too late, and the animals behind them pushed them over. The bison did not jump. They fell. Below, hunters were waiting, and a wounded bison that still stood was not to be taken lightly.

Then came the hardest part. Brink estimates that a typical kill, of a hundred cows and fifty calves, weighed about sixty thousand kilograms, as much as twenty-five pickup trucks. Several hundred people had to turn it into food and hides before it spoiled, probably working around the clock. The camp stood on level ground beside a spring. Bison meat is mostly water, so it was cut into thin strips and dried, to be light enough to carry to a winter camp. The bones from nearly six thousand years of hunts lie at the site in layers about ten metres deep.

One rule drew the condemnation of many Europeans. Every animal driven to the cliff had to die, even when there was more meat than anyone could use. The Europeans called it waste. The hunters' reasoning was different. They believed that bison were aware, and remembered. An animal that escaped would carry the secret of the trap back to other herds, and the next hunt would fail. One traveler found bison skulls with sage stuffed into the eye sockets and nostrils, so that they could not warn the living herds. For the hunters, Brink writes, the killing continued so that the people themselves could continue.

The place has a name with a story. In the Blackfoot language, it is called Estipah-skikikini-kots. According to a tradition of the Piikani, one of the Blackfoot peoples, a young man too young to join the hunt wanted to watch the herd fall. He hid under a ledge at the foot of the cliff. The animals piled up until they reached his hiding place. When the hunters pulled them away, they found him crushed against the rock. Today, some Blackfoot elders say the story is true, but belongs to another jump, and the name was given to the wrong place. "We always just called it the buffalo jump," they told Brink.

The end came with horses. In the winter of 1792, the trader Peter Fidler watched Piikani riders try to drive herds over cliffs near Head-Smashed-In. The herds broke through the lines, and in one attempt, only a single animal fell. A system made for people on foot could not simply be adapted to riders. And with a horse, one hunter could kill a few bison on any day, without hundreds of people. By the middle of the 1800s, the great hunts at the jump had probably ended, and by 1880 the bison themselves were nearly gone. A photograph from 1912 still shows the slope below the cliff white with bones.

Today, Head-Smashed-In is a World Heritage Site, with a museum built into the cliff, shaped with the help of Piikani elders such as Joe Crowshoe.

A trick that worked for nearly six thousand years was not defeated by the bison. It ended when the hunters became faster.
