# Research — How Hunters With No Horse and No Gun Herded Bison Off a Cliff (stage 03)

TOPIC
New idea **#40** in `docs/ideas.md` (added in this session, out of the original 30): the buffalo jump at
Head-Smashed-In, Alberta — how people on foot, with no horse and no gun, moved herds of bison over a cliff for nearly
six thousand years. Chosen as the user's "ancient story of how people did something that is today completely normal"
(hunting; 2026-11-16, "from any part of the world"). First story from the Great Plains and first "how people lived"
story on the channel. Region: North America (the Plains); epoch: Ancient World to the 1800s (the practice runs for
~5,800 years). Popularity is **not measured**; the site's name is famous as a joke, the method is rarely told in detail.

RECOMMENDED STORY
The problem (animals ten times your weight, faster than you, with horns; no horse, no rifle). The place: a ~10 m
sandstone cliff at the edge of the Rockies' foothills, a gathering basin burned months in advance to draw herds, and
lines of stone cairns (hundreds, over ten kilometres of lanes) funnelling out from the edge. The hunt: ceremony; young
buffalo runners in wolf hides or calf skins, scented with sage and fat, downwind, moving like animals, bleating like a
lost calf so that cows follow; people hiding behind the cairns who stand up behind the herd; no stampede, but
patience, until the herd arrives as a tight pack; at the end a run downhill around a curve that hides the cliff; the
leaders see the edge too late and are pushed over by the animals behind; hunters and the camp below. The name:
the Piikani story of the young man who hid under a ledge to watch and was crushed; some elders say the story is true
but belongs to another jump. The end: horses; in 1792–93 Peter Fidler sees Piikani riders try to drive herds over cliffs
and the herds break through; a system built for people on foot cannot be bolted onto riders; the great hunts fade
by about the mid-1800s.

STORY ANGLE
Not "primitive hunters stampeded buffalo". The common picture (men running and waving) "would be close to zero chance"
(Brink). What worked was an exact knowledge of the animal: curiosity, a mother's response to a calf's call, the way a
herd follows its leaders, and the fact that a tight pack at the edge kills all and a strung-out line kills none. The
end is not the bison's disappearance but the hunters' new speed.

ONE-SENTENCE HOOK (verbatim into ideas.md and the script)
How do you move a herd of animals that weigh ten times what you do, run much faster than you, and have horns, when you have no horse and no rifle?

HISTORICAL PERIOD
The site's oldest layers date to about 5,800 years ago (Brink); used until perhaps the mid-1800s; recorded by Europeans
from the 1790s; excavated from 1938/1949; World Heritage Site 1981. For the site: **Ancient World** (the long span of
the practice) — the year is not a point, so the script gives the span.

LOCATION / CULTURAL CONTEXT
Head-Smashed-In Buffalo Jump, on the Porcupine Hills edge, ~18 km west of Fort Macleod, southern Alberta, Canada; the
Piikani (Peigan) and other Blackfoot peoples; the northern Plains.

STORY TYPE
Everyday life / technology: Problem → Old solution → Innovation → Adoption → Unexpected consequence → Impact.

KEY CHARACTERS
The buffalo runners (young men, unnamed); the hunters and the people behind the cairns; the Piikani young man of the
name story; Jack Brink (archaeologist, director of the digs for ten years); Peter Fidler (fur trader, 1792–93);
Weasel Tail (Joe Crowshoe Sr., Piikani elder, quoted by Ewers on women and children behind the willow piles).

CENTRAL STORY QUESTION
How could people on foot make a wild herd walk to a cliff?

CORE CONFLICT
People against a herd's instincts; then, the old technique against the horse.

TURNING POINT
The runners' trick: the lost calf.

PAYOFF
The system was ended not by failure but by a faster way to hunt.

KEY EVENTS (Brink, *Imagining Head-Smashed-In*, AU Press 2008, chapters 1, 4, 5, 6, 9)
1. **E** The place: ~300 m of cliff, ~10 m at the highest; a spring; the oldest layers ~5,800 BP, "one of the deepest sites
   in North America", bones about ten metres below the surface; possibly the oldest jump, or second to Bonfire Shelter
   (Texas, ~10,000 BP, disputed as a jump).
2. **E** Gathering basin burned "months in advance to encourage lush re-growth that attracted big herds" (National Trust
   of Canada); drive lanes "over 10 kilometres" (same), "some single lanes extending for many kilometres" (Brink);
   "hundreds of cairns" (Wikipedia); low platforms of stone, probably topped with sod, dung and brush (Brink, "Deadmen").
3. **E** "When buffalo were needed, the shaman invited several young men …" (Mandelbaum, Plains Cree, in Brink): the
   hunt was steeped in ceremony; the leader was credited with finding and calling the herd.
4. **E** Buffalo runners: "young men … instructed in the art of manoeuvring bison herds by the older buffalo runners";
   disguises of wolves, coyotes, calves; "rubbed their bodies with sage and animal fat"; "they didn't move as human
   beings; they moved as the animals they had become"; downwind; the calf's call: "Even if she knows her own calf is
   nearby, maternal instinct dictates that every cow will investigate" (Brink).
5. **E** The drive: "dozens, probably hundreds, of people, often including women and older children" along the lanes
   (Brink); Weasel Tail to Ewers: a man on the hill signalled "the women and children, who were hiding behind the
   willow piles"; Alexander Henry: bison are "in a manner enticed to their destruction"; short bursts and lulls; "a full
   stampede any distance from the edge of the cliff was undesirable".
6. **E** The edge: a downhill run and a curve that hid the cliff; leaders "sensed the imminent danger" and tried to
   turn; "the mass of animals following immediately behind crashed into them"; "if a line of two hundred bison arrives at
   the edge of a cliff, you end up killing none of them, but if a pack of two hundred arrives, you kill them all" — Brink
   calls it a theory without hard data. "Bison didn't jump … They fell, they stumbled, they sailed, they rolled."
7. **E** Below: hunters with spears and clubs; a camp with fresh water; butchering; hides, bones, pemmican (Brink ch. 7–8).
8. **T** The name Estipah-skikikini-kots: a Piikani tradition; the young man "too young to be involved in the immediate
   chase and kill" hid under a ledge; the carcasses piled up to his hiding place; he was found "crushed against the cliff,
   his head smashed in". Brink: the iron-red patches on the cliff are rust, "not blood". Many Blackfoot say the story is
   right but the place is wrong (a jump on Willow Creek); the elders' answer to Brink: "We always just called it the buffalo jump."
   Fidler's map (1801, from a Blackfoot informant): "Steep rocks where buffalo fall before and break their skulls all to pieces."
9. **E** The end: Fidler, winter 1792–93 with the Piikani near the site: horse-mounted men "brought a herd of Buffalo to
   the rock but they all broke out at the outer end of the Dead Men & only one single one fell over the precipice"; "the
   hybrid failed the Piikani"; the horse "made the whole world of the Aboriginal people smaller"; mass kills faded
   "from about the mid-1700s to mid-1800s"; Head-Smashed-In "continued to be used, only very occasionally, until perhaps the
   middle of the 1800s"; by 1880 the bison were nearly extinct; a 1912 photograph shows the slope still white with bones.
10. **E** 1968 national historic site; 1981 UNESCO World Heritage Site; interpretive centre opened 1987, built into the
    cliff with Piikani elders (Joe Crowshoe Sr.).

11. **E** The nose of the buffalo: bison "can smell ... as much as several kilometres away"; "hardly any part of their daily
    activities ... takes place without frequent reference to what is in the wind"; "precious few kill events took place in
    prehistory without reference to what the wind was doing" (Brink ch. 5).
12. **E** Other runners' tricks: moving, falling, standing still to rouse the herd's curiosity; Kane (1840s): hunters crawling
    in single file, which "the Indians account for by saying that the buffalo supposes it to be a big snake".
13. **E** Size and work of a kill (Brink ch. 6-7): "if an average kill consisted of about one hundred cows ... and another fifty
    calves and juveniles ... about 60,000 kilograms ... twenty-five full-sized pickup trucks"; "several hundred people";
    "probably working around the clock"; processing on the flats below the cliff, at least a kilometre long; water from the
    spring; ~65 per cent of the meat's weight is water, so it was cut into thin strips and dried to be carried; autumn
    was probably the main season, the winter camp elsewhere.
14. **E** The rule: "All the bison had to be killed because they now knew too much"; Henry (c. 1800): "not one of the whole
    herd do they allow to escape ... to prevent alarming other herds"; Bradbury: skulls with sage in the eye sockets and
    nostrils "to prevent them from apprising the living buffaloes of the danger"; Brink: "The killing continued so that
    the people themselves could continue." Also a practical reason: wounded, enraged bison were a danger.

CAUSES
Survival on the Plains without a horse; one of several mass-kill techniques (jumps, pounds, surrounds).

CONSEQUENCES
Food, hides and tools for hundreds of people from one kill; later, the horse made the communal kill unnecessary.

MOST INTERESTING FACTS
- The disguise includes a *sound* — a lost calf's call — chosen because it works on cows, the target.
- The red streaks on the cliff are not blood.
- The people behind the lanes included women and children.
- The elders say the place with that name may not be this one.

MYTHS / LEGENDS / TRADITIONS
The name story is a Piikani tradition, kept in oral history; the script tells it as a tradition. Shaman and ceremony
are mentioned once; details of Blackfoot ceremony are **not** invented.

UNCERTAINTIES / DEBATES
1. **Age:** 5,500 (Wikipedia), ~5,800 BP (Brink), "almost 6,000" (a site guide), ~3900 BC (another page). The script says "nearly six thousand".
2. **Where the name belongs:** the Blackfoot elders' objection (Brink) — told once.
3. **Waste:** Wikipedia says the practice has been "criticized as highly wasteful"; Brink devotes a chapter to the
   question ("Overkill?"): the hunters killed every animal brought to the trap because, in their belief, survivors would
   warn other herds and doom future hunts, and because wounded animals were dangerous. **In the script from the
   10-minute version on** (one paragraph, no defence and no accusation); the Europeans' condemnation is attributed, not endorsed.
4. **The run itself:** "there are no living eyewitnesses" (Brink); the reconstruction rests on archaeology, Fidler,
   Lewis and Clark, and Blackfoot testimony. The script's drive is the reconstruction, not an eyewitness account of
   Head-Smashed-In. One sentence of the script says "archaeologists think".
5. **Runners' disguises** are documented by 19th-century witnesses (Henry 1809, Audubon, Catlin, Kane, Lewis); the script
   states them as the method, as Brink does.
6. **Not used:** two reference-site mirrors with invented text (`presentationbuilder…`, `cavac.at`).

RECOMMENDED VOICE
Explorer / discovery.

VOICE CHARACTER
Warm, curious, approachable, with respect for the people who did this; neither triumphant nor apologetic.

VOICE DELIVERY
~143 wpm. The hook as a real question, then a beat. "They persuaded it" soft. The calf's bleat is described, not
imitated. "Fell" plain. The name story slow and quiet. The last two sentences level.

WHY THIS VOICE FITS
The story is a piece of intelligence, not a battle. An epic voice would turn it into a stampede; a dark voice would
make a hunt into a massacre; the explorer voice keeps the curiosity.
