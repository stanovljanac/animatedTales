Three brothers drew lots for the universe. Their three sisters were never given a lot.

All six had the same father. Cronus had been warned that one of his children would overthrow him, so he swallowed each of them at birth — the daughters first: Hestia, Demeter and Hera. Only the youngest, Zeus, was hidden from him. Grown, he forced his father to bring them all back up, alive. Together they beat the Titans. Then the brothers divided the prize: the sky to Zeus, the sea to Poseidon, the dead to Hades. The full story of that draw is in the previous part.

The sisters got no kingdom. They got something smaller, and closer to home: a marriage, a harvest, and a fire in the middle of the house.

Hera married the winner.

She became queen of the gods and goddess of marriage — married to the least faithful husband in Greek myth. She could not punish Zeus, so she went after the women he loved, and their children. Once she had Zeus put to sleep and sent a storm after his son Heracles. Zeus woke in a rage. He hung her from the sky, a golden chain on her wrists and two anvils tied to her feet.

And yet the Greeks never treated her as the loser. At Olympia, Zeus's own sanctuary, her temple stood for more than a century before his. And in one town in Arcadia she had three shrines: to Hera the Girl, Hera the Wife, and Hera the Widow — from the time she quarrelled with Zeus and went home.

A widow, with a husband who could never die.

Demeter got the grain.

With Zeus she had a daughter, Persephone. One day the girl was picking flowers when the ground split open, and Hades drove up in a golden chariot and carried her down to the dead. Zeus, her own father, had promised her to him — without a word to her mother.

For nine days Demeter searched the earth with a torch in each hand, without eating or washing. Then the Sun, who sees everything, told her the truth. Hades was her own brother, he said, a fitting husband — lord of the third share he drew when the world was divided.

She did not accept it. She left Olympus, sat down in her temple at Eleusis, and stopped the grain.

For a whole year nothing grew; oxen dragged ploughs through the fields for nothing. Mankind was starving, and the gods were about to lose their sacrifices. Zeus sent a messenger, then every god on Olympus, one after another, bearing gifts. She refused them all. Nothing would grow until she saw her daughter.

And the king of the sky gave in. He sent Hermes to bring Persephone back, and Hades let her go — but first he slipped her a single pomegranate seed. She had eaten in the land of the dead, so for a third of every year she would return below. Only then did Demeter let the fields grow again.

The sister with no kingdom had made the king of the sky back down, and the king of the dead give back what he had taken.

Hestia got the fire.

She was the first child Cronus swallowed, and the last to come back up. Poseidon and Apollo both asked to marry her. She laid her hand on Zeus's head and swore never to marry at all. So instead of a husband, Zeus gave her a place: the middle of every house.

The first offering at every feast went to her, and the last. Greek cities kept a public hearth, and when colonists sailed off to found a new city, they carried its fire with them. She has almost no myths of her own. In Plato, when the gods ride out across heaven, Hestia alone stays home.

The brothers drew three places where no living person can stay. The sisters got the places where people live.

Every human life ends in Hades' kingdom. It is lived in theirs.
