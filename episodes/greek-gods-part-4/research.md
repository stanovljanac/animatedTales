# Research — Greek Gods, Part 4: Hephaestus, Aphrodite, Ares (stage 03)

SERIES POSITION
Part 4 of 4, the last numbered part. Spine, locked descriptions, the outro chain and the material
boundaries against Parts 1–3 live in `docs/series/greek-gods.md`. This file researches **only
Part 4**. Part 3 ended on Hera, who "could not stop a child born from her husband's head"; Part 4
opens there. The recap is two sentences and is carried by two Part 3 shots that already exist
(shot 12, Athena leaping from the head; shot 20, Hera on Olympus). Hesiod puts the two births side
by side (*Theogony* 924–929): Zeus bears Athena alone, Hera bears Hephaestus alone — that pairing is
the bridge.

TOPIC
Hephaestus, the god his mother threw away, and the net he made for his wife and his brother.

RECOMMENDED STORY
Hera, furious that Zeus bore Athena alone, bears Hephaestus alone. He is born lame; she is ashamed
of him and throws him off Olympus. Sea goddesses take him in, and for nine years nobody on Olympus
knows he is alive. A later tradition has him take revenge with a golden throne that traps his
mother; only the god of wine can talk him into coming back. Olympus then needs him — he builds the
gods' houses and golden servants that can think. He marries Aphrodite, who loves his brother Ares.
Helios tells him; he forges chains too fine to see, pretends to leave for Lemnos, and the chains
close on the two lovers. He calls the gods to look. They laugh, Hermes jokes that he would take the
chains three times over, Poseidon pledges to pay Ares' fine, and Hephaestus lets them go.

STORY ANGLE
**The god who could not chase anyone, so he built traps.** The same wound opens both halves of the
story: his mother rejects him for being lame, and his wife scorns him for being lame — Hephaestus
says so himself in the Odyssey. Both times his answer is a thing that holds. Both times it works,
and both times he has to let go. The gods themselves name the irony in the Odyssey: "the slow one
has caught the swift."

ONE-SENTENCE HOOK
Hera threw her own son off Olympus for being lame — and he answered with chains no one could see.

HISTORICAL PERIOD
Myth, no date. Textual anchors: Hesiod, *Theogony* (c. 700 BCE); Homer, *Iliad* (c. 750–700 BCE) and
*Odyssey* (c. 725–675 BCE); *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* (7th–6th c. BCE); Pausanias (2nd c. CE) for the
golden throne; Hyginus, *Fabulae* 166 (2nd c. CE) and Libanius, *Narrations* 7 (4th c. CE) as later
witnesses to it.

LOCATION / CULTURAL CONTEXT
Archaic Greek epic. Named places: Olympus, the sea cave of Thetis and Eurynome (no place name in
Homer), Lemnos (the pretext), Thrace (Ares' home), Paphos on Cyprus (Aphrodite's).

STORY TYPE
Myth / religion — World → Figure → Origin → Role → Conflict → Main story → Meaning. The net is the
main story and is told as a *song inside the poem*: in the Odyssey the blind bard Demodocus sings it
at a king's feast (8.266–366) and Odysseus enjoys it (8.367–369).

KEY CHARACTERS
Hephaestus, Aphrodite, Ares. Present in one or two lines each: Hera (the mother), Zeus (called to
the doorway), Helios (the informant), Poseidon (the pledge), Apollo and Hermes (the audience).
Unnamed: the two sea goddesses (Thetis and Eurynome) and the god of wine (Dionysus) — one hard name
fewer each for the voice and the viewer, and both belong to other stories.

CENTRAL STORY QUESTION
What does a god do who can never outrun anyone?

CORE CONFLICT
The lame son against the people who reject him for it: first his mother, then his wife and brother.

TURNING POINT
The chains close. Everything before it is waiting — the throwing, the cave, the courtship, Helios's
report, the forging. From that moment the story turns from the trap to what the trap was worth.

PAYOFF
The net works perfectly and buys him nothing but an audience. The gods call it a triumph of craft
over speed, one of them says he would gladly take the chains himself, Poseidon pays the fine on
Ares' behalf if it is ever owed, and Hephaestus has to say yes to the god of the sea. Twice he builds a trap — a throne for his mother, a net for his wife — and twice, in the
end, he lets them go.

KEY EVENTS

Evidence labels per `channel-bible.md` §3. For a myth episode: what a named ancient text says

**Birth and fall**
1. Zeus bears Athena from his head; Hera, "angry and at odds with her husband", bears famous
   Hephaestus without union with Zeus, "who excelled all the sons of Heaven in craft". [TRADITION —
   *Theogony* 924–929] Homer instead makes him the son of Zeus and Hera. The script follows Hesiod
   and says "Hesiod says"; it never claims a father either way.
2. Hera is ashamed of his lameness. In the Iliad Hephaestus tells it himself: his mother "wished to
   hide" him because he was lame (18.395–397). In the Hymn to Apollo she calls him weak and
   shrivelled of foot, a shame to her among the gods, and says she took him in her hands and threw
   him into the sea. [TRADITION — *Iliad* 18.395–397; *Hymn to Apollo* 3.316–321]
3. Thetis and Eurynome catch him and take him into their care; he spends nine years in a hollow
   cave making jewellery — brooches, spiral armlets, rosettes, necklaces — with the Ocean murmuring
   around it. No one else, god or man, knew. [TRADITION — *Iliad* 18.398–409]
4. **Variant, not used:** in *Iliad* 1.586–594 it is Zeus who throws him, for taking Hera's side; he
   falls all day and lands on Lemnos, where the Sintians tend him. Apollodorus runs the two throws
   together; later writers make the second fall the cause of his lameness, while Homer already has
   him lame at birth. The script tells Hera's throw only. [TRADITION, variant]

**The throne**
5. Hephaestus sends Hera a golden chair with invisible fetters; once she sits, she cannot rise. He
   will not come to Olympus to release her and listens to no god except Dionysus, who makes him
   drunk and brings him up. [TRADITION, late — Pausanias 1.20.3; Hyginus, *Fabulae* 166; Libanius,
   *Narrations* 7 adds that Ares tried to free Hera first and was driven off with torches] Plato
   already knew the story and disliked it (*Republic* 378d). Attic vase painting of Dionysus leading
   Hephaestus home begins in the early 6th century BCE; the François Vase (c. 570 BCE) is the usual
   example. [RECORD of art — cited from memory, not re-checked]. The script says "a later tradition".

**Olympus's craftsman**
6. He built each god's house on Olympus (*Iliad* 1.606–608) and made twenty bellows that work
   by themselves (18.468–473), tripods that roll to the assembly alone (18.373–377), and golden
   maidens "with minds in their hearts, and speech and strength" (18.417–420). [TRADITION]
7. His wife varies: Aphrodite in the Odyssey (8.266–366), Charis in the Iliad (18.382–383),
   Aglaea, youngest of the Graces, in Hesiod (*Theogony* 945–946). [TRADITION, variant] The script
   follows the Odyssey, because it is the only version that has a story.

**Ares**
8. Ares is Hera's son by Zeus (*Theogony* 921–923), so Hephaestus's brother (half-brother at the
   very least in every version). Zeus tells him to his face that he is the most hateful of all the
   gods on Olympus, because strife and wars are all he loves (*Iliad* 5.889–891). [TRADITION]
9. Two giants, Otus and Ephialtes, chained him in a bronze jar for thirteen months; he would have
   died if Hermes had not stolen him out (*Iliad* 5.385–391). [TRADITION] This is why the script
   can call him "the god who had been chained before" and why Hermes' joke later is a small echo.

**The net** (Odyssey 8.266–366, sung by Demodocus)
10. Helios saw Ares and Aphrodite together and told Hephaestus (8.270–271). [TRADITION]
11. Hephaestus goes to his anvil and makes bonds that cannot be broken or slipped, "fine as
    spider-webs" so that not even the blessed gods could see them (8.274–281), lets them fall around
    the bedposts and hang from the roof-beams, and pretends he is going to Lemnos (8.283). The web
    simile is in the Greek (8.280, *arachnia lepta*); some translations drop it. [TRADITION]
12. Ares keeps a sharp watch, sees him go and comes at once; they lie down; the bonds close and they
    cannot lift or move a limb (8.285–299). [TRADITION]
13. Hephaestus turns back before Lemnos and shouts to Zeus and all the gods to come and see "deeds
    laughable and not to be borne": Aphrodite always dishonours him because he is lame and loves
    Ares because Ares is handsome and sound of foot, while he himself was born feeble — his two
    parents are to blame, he wishes they had never begotten him. He will keep the pair bound until
    Zeus returns the bride-gifts he paid. [TRADITION — 8.301–320]
14. Poseidon, Hermes and Apollo come; the goddesses stay home out of modesty (8.321–324). Laughter
    that cannot be put out breaks over the gods as they look at the craft of Hephaestus (8.326–327),
    and one says to his neighbour: "Bad deeds do not prosper. The slow catches the swift — as now
    slow Hephaestus has caught Ares, swiftest of the gods who hold Olympus, lame as he is, by craft."
    (8.329–332) [TRADITION]
15. Apollo asks Hermes whether he would take such strong chains for a night with golden Aphrodite;
    Hermes says he would, three times as many, with all the gods and goddesses watching
    (8.334–342). [TRADITION — checked in several translations, not on the theoi page]
16. Only Poseidon does not laugh. He begs Hephaestus to release Ares and promises he will pay the
    fine; Hephaestus answers that a pledge for a worthless man is worthless; Poseidon says that if
    Ares runs without paying, he will pay it himself; Hephaestus yields (8.343–359). Ares goes to
    Thrace, Aphrodite to Paphos in Cyprus (8.361–363). [TRADITION]
17. Hesiod lists Phobos (Fear), Deimos (Panic) and Harmonia as the children of Aphrodite and Ares
    (*Theogony* 933–937). He does not date them against the net. The script puts them next to the
    release and claims no order. [TRADITION; the placing is the narrator's, INTERPRETATION]

MOST INTERESTING FACTS
- **Two births, side by side.** Zeus bears Athena alone, Hera answers by bearing Hephaestus alone —
  Hesiod sets them in consecutive lines.
- **The god tells his own wound.** In the Iliad Hephaestus says to Thetis that his mother wanted to
  hide him because he was lame; in the Odyssey he says his wife scorns him for the same reason.
- **Nine years in a cave** making jewellery, and nobody on Olympus knew.
- **A throne that holds the queen of heaven**, and the only god he will listen to is the god of wine.
- **Golden servants that can think and speak** — the Iliad's version of a robot.
- **The Greeks told the net as entertainment.** A blind bard sings it at a feast; Odysseus enjoys it.
- **The gods say the moral themselves**: "The slow catches the swift."
- **The god of war had been chained before** — in a bronze jar, for thirteen months.
- **Hermes' joke**: three times the chains, with every god watching.
- **Poseidon is the only god who does not laugh**, and ends up as guarantor for the fine.
- **War and love have three children**: Fear, Panic and Harmony.

MYTHS / LEGENDS / TRADITIONS
The whole episode is tradition and is told as a story inside it. The narration steps outside four
times, each attributed: "Hesiod says…" (Hera bears him alone), "A later tradition says…" (the
throne), "Homer's Odyssey tells…" (the song at the feast, framing the net), "Hesiod gives…" (the
three children). The nine years in the cave and the golden servants are told straight.

UNCERTAINTIES / DEBATES
- **Who fathered him.** Zeus and Hera in Homer; Hera alone in Hesiod. The script says only what
  Hesiod says and never names a father.
- **Who threw him.** Hera (the Hymn, Iliad 18) or Zeus (Iliad 1). Script: Hera.
- **Who his wife is.** Aphrodite (Odyssey), Charis (Iliad), Aglaea (Hesiod). Script: Aphrodite.
- **Aphrodite's origin.** Daughter of Zeus (Odyssey 8.308, 8.320; Iliad 5.370 adds Dione) or born
  from the foam (Hesiod, *Theogony* 188–206). The foam birth drags in Uranus and the sickle, which
  the series bible reserves for the Titan video. The script tells neither: Zeus appears only as the
  god who received the bride-gifts, and her parentage is never stated.
- **"Ugly."** The series bible's spine first said Hera threw him off because he was ugly. No ancient
  text says that: they say *lame* (*cholos*), *weak* (*epedanos*), *shrivelled of foot*. The bible
  has been corrected to *lame* (§2, §4.7), and the script follows the sources.
- **The throne story is late.** Pausanias, Hyginus and Libanius are 2nd–4th century CE, with a lost
  Pindar fragment and vase painting from the 6th century BCE behind them. Homer does not mention it.
- **Aphrodite and Ares' children against the net.** Hesiod gives no order. The script claims none.

AVOIDED ON PURPOSE
- **The Cyclopes at the forge**, and Etna as his workshop — Hellenistic and Roman (Callimachus,
  Virgil), not archaic.
- **Pandora** (*Theogony* 560 ff.) and the rest of the Prometheus cycle — its own episode, and about
  creation, not about the net.
- **Athena and Hephaestus** (the attempted assault, Erichthonius) — Part 3 avoided Athena's
  virginity story on purpose, and this episode does not reopen it.
- **The armour of Achilles** (*Iliad* 18.478 ff.) and Thetis's request — a strong scene, but it is
  Achilles' story and would add a third mother who protects him.
- **The Sintians and Lemnos** — only the pretext survives.
- **Aphrodite's other loves** (Anchises, Adonis) and **Ares and Athena's fights** (*Iliad* 5, 21) —
  separate stories.
- **Ares' fall to Diomedes** (*Iliad* 5.855–863) — a different humiliation of the same god.
- **Dionysus and Hermes as characters.** Both are named only by role or in one line; they belong to
  a Part 5 that is not planned (series bible §2).
- **Aphrodite's birth, Uranus and the sickle** — Titan video (series bible §7).

BRAND-SAFETY NOTE FOR THE VISUAL STAGE
The Odyssey places the lovers in a bed. The narration says "lay down together" and never more. Every
bed frame with Ares and Aphrodite has the **cover drawn up to the shoulders, heads and faces above
it**, with the net falling over the cover — no bare body is ever shown. Outside the bed they are
dressed as locked. This note is for the storyboard; it changes nothing in the script.

RECOMMENDED VOICE
Ancient / mysterious — the same voice as Parts 1, 2 and 3.

VOICE CHARACTER
Deep, atmospheric, deliberate, slightly dark. The same narrator, continuing the same account.

VOICE DELIVERY
~157 wpm, the pace Part 3 was delivered at. Even and flat through the recap and the fall: "She
threw him off Olympus" as a fact. Dry, a little wry for the throne. Slow, unhurried build for the net
— "fine as spider webs" with room around it, a beat before "and the chains closed around them." The
shout from the doorway is the one place the voice rises, hurt rather than triumphant. Light and a
little wicked for the two gods' exchange; the laughter breaks the tension. The last three
sentences slow and unpushed, a full beat before "And both times, in the end, he let them go."

WHY THIS VOICE FITS
Series continuity is not optional — the same narrator who told the draw, the sisters and the
births. The material also wants restraint: a mother who throws her son away, a net, a joke at
a cuckold's expense. Told evenly and without a smirk, the cruelty lands and the humour lands harder.
