Zeus had swallowed his wife, and his daughter leapt out of his head in golden armour. Hera could not stop a child born from her husband's head.

So, Hesiod says, she answered in anger. She bore a son of her own, without Zeus — Hephaestus, who would become the greatest craftsman among the gods.

But he was born lame, and Hera was ashamed of him. She threw him off Olympus.

Two sea goddesses caught him as he fell. For nine years he lived in a cave beneath the waves, making jewellery for them, and no other god knew he was alive.

A later tradition says he took his revenge. When he was grown, he sent his mother a gift: a golden throne. Hera sat down, and invisible chains closed around her. She could not stand.

Hephaestus refused to come to Olympus and free her. He would listen to no god except one — the god of wine, who made him drunk and led him up the mountain. Hera went free.

In time, Olympus needed him. He built the houses the gods lived in, and servants of gold that could think, and speak.

He married Aphrodite, the most beautiful of the goddesses. Homer's Odyssey tells what came next, in a song a blind poet sings at a king's feast.

Aphrodite loved another: Ares, the god of war. He was Hera's other son — Hephaestus's own brother — the swiftest god on Olympus, and the one Zeus said he hated most. Once, two giants had chained him in a bronze jar for thirteen months, until Hermes stole him out.

But the Sun sees everything. Helios saw the two of them together, and went straight to Hephaestus.

Hephaestus went to his forge. He hammered out chains as fine as spider webs — too fine for any god to see, too strong for any god to break. He fixed them around his bed, and hung them from the roof beams. Then he announced that he was leaving for Lemnos.

Ares had been watching the house. He came at once. The two lay down together — and the chains closed around them, and neither could lift a limb.

Hephaestus turned back before Lemnos. He stood in his doorway and called for Zeus and every god to come and see something laughable and unbearable. Aphrodite, he said, had always scorned him for his lame leg, and loved Ares because Ares was handsome and sound of foot. He demanded that Zeus give back every gift he had paid for her.

The goddesses stayed at home, out of shame. The gods came — Poseidon, Apollo and Hermes. Apollo asked Hermes whether he would take such chains for a night with golden Aphrodite. Hermes said he would take three times as many, with every god looking on.

The gods roared with laughter. "Bad deeds do not prosper," one said. "The slow one has caught the swift. Lame as he is, Hephaestus has caught the swiftest god on Olympus — by craft."

Only Poseidon did not laugh. He begged Hephaestus to let Ares go, and promised that if Ares ran without paying, Poseidon himself would pay. Hephaestus could not refuse him, and undid the chains.

Ares fled to Thrace. Aphrodite went to Cyprus. Hesiod gives the two of them three children: Fear, Panic and Harmony.

Hephaestus could never outrun anyone, so twice he built a trap instead: a throne for his mother, and a net for his wife. Both times he caught them. And both times, in the end, he let them go.
