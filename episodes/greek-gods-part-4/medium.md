# Hephaestus, Aphrodite and Ares: The Lame God Who Answered Rejection With Traps

[![Hephaestus, Aphrodite and Ares caught in the net, a scene from the 2D animated story]({{THUMBNAIL}})]({{VIDEO_URL}})

The most famous joke in Greek mythology was sung at a feast. In Book 8 of Homer's *Odyssey*, a blind bard named Demodocus stands before a king and his guests and performs a song about the gods. It is not a hymn. It is a scandal, and Odysseus, who is listening, enjoys every line of it.

The song is about a craftsman, his wife and his brother. To understand why it is funny, and why it is also a little sad, you have to start with how the craftsman came into the world.

## Two births, side by side

Hesiod's *Theogony*, composed around 700 BCE, places two births in consecutive lines. Zeus brings Athena out of his own head. Hera, angry and at odds with her husband, answers by bearing a child without him: Hephaestus, who would outdo every other son of Heaven in craft. Homer disagrees on the fatherhood, since his Hephaestus is the son of Zeus and Hera together. The story below follows Hesiod and claims no father at all.

The sources agree on what went wrong next. The child was lame. In the *Iliad*, Hephaestus tells the story himself: his mother wished to hide him because of his lameness. The *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* is harsher, with Hera calling her son weak and shrivelled of foot, a shame to her among the gods, and taking him in her hands to throw him into the sea.

Note the word the ancient texts use. Later retellings sometimes say Hera discarded him for being ugly. No ancient source says so. They say lame, weak, shrivelled of foot, and that distinction matters, because the whole story that follows grows out of one wound.

## Nine years under the waves

Two sea goddesses, Thetis and Eurynome, catch the falling god and raise him in a hollow cave. According to the *Iliad*, he spent nine years there making jewellery for them: brooches, spiral armlets, rosettes, necklaces, with the Ocean murmuring around the rock. No one else, god or mortal, knew he was alive.

Then comes a tale that Homer never tells. Writers of the 2nd to 4th centuries CE, among them Pausanias and Hyginus, describe a golden throne that Hephaestus sends to Hera as a gift. She sits, and invisible fetters hold her fast. Hephaestus will not come to Olympus to release her. Only Dionysus, the god of wine, can persuade him, by making him drunk and leading him up the mountain. Plato already knew this story and disliked it, which suggests it is much older than the surviving texts that tell it.

It is a late tradition, and it should be labelled as one. But it fits the pattern of the figure so neatly that it is hard to leave out: a god who cannot chase anyone builds something that holds.

## The craftsman Olympus could not do without

The gods came to need him. In the *Iliad* he builds each god's house on Olympus. He makes twenty bellows that work on their own, tripods that roll to the divine assembly without a hand to push them, and golden maidens with minds in their hearts, with speech and strength. It is as close as archaic Greek epic comes to describing robots.

His marriage is where the sources split. The *Odyssey* gives him Aphrodite. The *Iliad* names Charis as his wife, and Hesiod names Aglaea, the youngest of the Graces. ## The Sun sees everything

Aphrodite, in the *Odyssey*, loves Ares, the god of war and Hephaestus's own brother. Zeus tells Ares to his face in the *Iliad* that he is the most hateful of all the gods on Olympus, since war and strife are all he loves. The same epic says two giants once held him in a bronze jar for thirteen months, and that he would have died there if Hermes had not stolen him out.

Helios, the Sun, sees the two lovers together and reports it to Hephaestus. The cuckolded husband does not storm Olympus. He goes to his anvil and forges bonds that can be neither broken nor slipped, as fine as spider webs, so fine that not even the blessed gods can see them. The Greek text has the spider-web comparison in it, though some translations drop it. He lets the chains fall around the bedposts and hang from the roof-beams, then announces that he is leaving for Lemnos.

Ares has been keeping a sharp watch on the house. He arrives at once. The chains close, and neither of the two can lift a limb.

## Come and see

Hephaestus turns back before he reaches Lemnos. He stands in his doorway and calls for Zeus and every god to come and look at something laughable and not to be borne. Aphrodite, he says, has always dishonoured him because he is lame, and loves Ares because Ares is handsome and sound of foot. He will keep the pair bound until Zeus returns the gifts he paid for his bride.

The goddesses stay home out of modesty. Poseidon, Hermes and Apollo come to look. The laughter that breaks over them cannot be put out. One god turns to his neighbour with the moral of the story: bad deeds do not prosper, and the slow has caught the swift. Lame as he is, Hephaestus has caught the swiftest god on Olympus by craft.

Apollo asks Hermes whether he would take such chains for a night with golden Aphrodite. Hermes answers that he would take three times as many, with every god and goddess watching.

Only Poseidon does not laugh. He begs Hephaestus to release Ares and promises that if Ares runs without paying the fine, Poseidon himself will pay it. Hephaestus first answers that a pledge for a worthless man is worth nothing, then gives way to the god of the sea and undoes the chains. Ares goes to Thrace, and Aphrodite to Paphos on Cyprus. Hesiod lists three children for the pair: Phobos, Deimos and Harmonia, Fear, Panic and Harmony. He does not say when they were born in relation to the net, so neither will this account.

## What the net was worth

Look at the story again from the end. Twice, Hephaestus builds a trap instead of a weapon, a throne for his mother and a net for his wife. Both times it works exactly as designed. Both times it buys him little beyond an audience, and both times he lets his captives go.

The Greeks seem to have understood this. They told the story of the net as entertainment, sang it at feasts, and put the moral in the mouths of the gods themselves. Nobody in the *Odyssey* pretends the lame god was wrong to be angry. They just notice that being right, in front of everyone, is not the same as winning.
