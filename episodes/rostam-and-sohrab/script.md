Rostam, the greatest hero of Persia, fought a young stranger on a battlefield, and twice the stranger asked him his name. Rostam would not tell him.

It began with a lost horse. While Rostam slept on a hunting trip, riders from Turan, Persia's enemy, stole his horse, Rakhsh, and took it to the city of Samangan. Rostam followed the tracks on foot. The king of Samangan, afraid of his anger, promised to find the horse and gave him a bed for the night. In the dark, the king's daughter, Tahmineh, came to his room. She had heard of his deeds, and she wanted a son like him. They were married, and Rakhsh was returned. Rostam gave her a jeweled armband: if she had a daughter, it was to be tied in her hair, and if a son, on his arm. Then he rode away, and he told no one.

The son was named Sohrab. He grew faster than any child, and at ten he demanded to know who his father was. Tahmineh told him, and begged him to keep it secret, because the king of Turan, Afrasiab, hated Rostam. Sohrab would not be silent. He would lead an army against Kay Kavus, the king of Iran, and put his father on the throne.

Afrasiab saw his chance. He sent his own warriors with Sohrab, with a secret order: Rostam must never learn who was coming against him. If the son killed his father, Iran would be defenseless. If the father killed his son, grief would finish him.

Sohrab's army marched into Iran, and came to the White Castle. Its commander, Hujir, rode out to face him, and was beaten and taken prisoner. Then a rider in armor came out to challenge Sohrab, and fought him with arrows and a lance, and nearly threw him from his horse. Sohrab chased the rider down and tore off the helmet, and long dark hair fell out. It was a woman, Gordafarid, the daughter of an old knight of the castle. He tied her with his cord, but she told him the army had seen her fight, and would laugh at him if he showed her as a prisoner. He believed her, and followed her to the castle gate. She slipped inside, and shut the door in his face.

In Iran, the king, Kay Kavus, sent for Rostam. A messenger rode to the hero's house with a letter: if you are holding roses, do not stop to smell them. Rostam read it, and asked about the young leader of Turan. He thought of his own son, in Samangan, and dismissed it. The boy was a child, Tahmineh had written. Then he feasted the messenger for three days. When he finally arrived, the king was furious, and ordered him hanged. Rostam answered that he was a free man and no slave, and that the king was nothing without him. He turned to leave the country. It took the nobles to persuade him back, and the king himself came down from his throne to ask his pardon.

On the night before the battle, Rostam crept into the enemy camp in disguise. Tahmineh's brother, who had been sent along with Sohrab so he could point out Rostam, challenged him, and Rostam struck him dead. In the morning, Sohrab questioned his prisoner, Hujir, and asked him to name the tents of the Persian camp. Three times he asked about the green tent. Each time, Hujir said he did not know it, hoping to protect Rostam. In the end, Sohrab killed him.

Then the two champions met between the camps. Rostam looked at the young man, and felt pity. "The air is warm and soft," he said, "but the earth is cold." He told him to leave the war. Sohrab looked at him and asked, "Are you Rostam?" Rostam answered, "I am not. Rostam is a champion. I am a slave." He said it so that Sohrab would believe Persia hid even greater strength. They fought until their weapons broke, and neither won.

The next morning, Sohrab asked again. He offered peace: to lay down their arms and drink wine together. Rostam said they had come to fight, not to talk. That evening, Sohrab threw him to the ground and drew his sword. Rostam said it was against the law of combat to kill a man the first time he falls. Sohrab let him go.

Rostam prayed by a stream. He was given so much strength that the rock under his feet gave way, and he asked for part of it to be taken back. Then he saw Sohrab coming, and asked for it again. When they met, he threw Sohrab, broke his back, and drew his sword. And Sohrab, dying, said: "I came looking for my father. Even if you became a fish in the sea, or a star in the sky, he would find you and avenge me. My father is Rostam." Rostam opened the young man's armor, and saw the armband.

Sohrab asked him for one thing: not to let the king punish the army of Turan, which had followed him only because he asked. Rostam promised, and sent word to Turan's general to take the army home. Then he sent to the king for a healing balm. Kay Kavus refused, afraid that a father and a son together would be stronger than he was. Sohrab died before help could come. Rostam burned his own tent, his saddle and his armor, and the nobles stopped him from taking his own life. Sohrab was buried in a tomb in the shape of a horse's hoof. Tahmineh, hearing the news, burned her son's house and gave away his gold, and within a year she was dead.

The poet Ferdowsi finished the Shahnameh, the Book of Kings, around the year 1010, and he tells this as a story from a legendary age. In 1853, the English poet Matthew Arnold retold it as Sohrab and Rustum. Almost everyone in it hides something, and almost everyone has a reason: pride, protection, ambition, fear. Together, those reasons made a father kill his son.
