# Research — The Hero Who Killed His Own Son Without Knowing It (stage 03)

TOPIC
New idea **#39** in `docs/ideas.md` (added in this session, out of the original 30): "Rostam and Sohrab", the best-known
tragedy of the *Shahnameh* (Ferdowsi, completed c. 1010 CE). First Persian / Iranian story on the channel. Chosen as
the user's "fable / invented story / myth that is interesting" (2026-11-16): a legend told as a legend, not as record.
Popularity is **not measured**; the story is a core text of the Persian-speaking world and is known in English mostly
through Matthew Arnold's poem *Sohrab and Rustum* (1853), but it is rarely told on history channels.

RECOMMENDED STORY
Rostam, Iran's greatest champion, loses his horse Rakhsh at Samangan in Turan and spends a night in the palace; the
king's daughter Tahmineh comes to his room, they marry, he gives her an armband (to be tied in a daughter's hair or on a
son's arm) and leaves, telling no one. Their son Sohrab grows impossibly fast, learns who his father is at ten, and
means to overthrow Kay Kavus and put his father on the throne of Iran. Afrasiab, king of Turan, sees a chance and
sends his own warriors with Sohrab, on the secret condition that Rostam never learns who is coming against him. At
the camp Rostam, disguised as a Turk, kills Zindeh, Tahmineh's brother, whom she had sent with Sohrab to point out the
father. Sohrab questions the captive Persian commander Hujir about the tents of the camp; Hujir, to protect Rostam,
lies three times and is killed. The duel: Rostam pities the boy ("the air is warm and soft, but the earth is cold");
Sohrab asks "are you Rostam?" and Rostam answers "I am not; I am a slave", so that Sohrab will believe Iran hides
more strength. They fight to a standstill. Next morning Sohrab asks again, offers peace and wine; Rostam refuses to
talk. That evening Sohrab throws him and draws his sword; Rostam claims a "law of combat" that a man may be killed
only the second time he falls, and Sohrab lets him go. Rostam prays, breaks Sohrab's back, and Sohrab, dying, says that
his father Rostam will avenge him; Rostam opens his armour and finds the armband. Rostam begs Kay Kavus for the healing
balm; the king refuses, fearing father and son together; Sohrab dies; Rostam burns his tent and gear; Tahmineh burns
her son's house and dies within a year.

STORY ANGLE
Not "fate". Almost everyone in the story withholds something, each with a reason that sounds good at the time (pride,
protection, ambition, fear, envy), and the sum of those reasons kills a son. The script lets the facts show it and says
it once at the end. The traditional telling ("it was written that it should be") is left in the characters' mouths.

ONE-SENTENCE HOOK (verbatim into ideas.md and the script)
Rostam, the greatest hero of Persia, fought a young stranger on a battlefield, and twice the stranger asked him his name. Rostam would not tell him.

HISTORICAL PERIOD
Legendary age of the *Shahnameh*, in the reign of Kay Kavus (no historical date); written down by Ferdowsi, completed
c. 1010 CE. For the site: **Myth & Legend**.

LOCATION / CULTURAL CONTEXT
The Iranian–Turanian frontier (Samangan, a White Castle, a field between two camps); Persian epic tradition.

STORY TYPE
Myth / legend; a tragedy of unrecognised identity. Myth structure: World → Figure → Origin → Conflict → Main story → Meaning.

KEY CHARACTERS
Rostam; Sohrab; Tahmineh; Afrasiab (king of Turan); Kay Kavus (king of Iran); Hujir (commander of the White Castle);
Zindeh (Tahmineh's brother); Human (Turanian general); the horse Rakhsh (Rakush).

CENTRAL STORY QUESTION
Why does Rostam not say his name — and what does it cost?

CORE CONFLICT
A boy looking for his father against a father hiding his name; and, around them, the lies of everyone who thinks
they are protecting someone.

TURNING POINT
The armband under the armour.

PAYOFF
A tragedy assembled out of reasons, not out of a villain alone.

KEY EVENTS (Helen Zimmern, *The Epic of Kings: Stories Retold from Firdusi*, 1883, pp. 129–171, full chapter read)
1. **T** Rakush stolen by seven knights of Turan; Rustem follows the tracks on foot to Samengan; the king soothes him;
   Tahmineh, "the Peri-faced", comes at night: "I am thine if thou wilt hear me"; marriage; "an onyx that was known unto
   all the world" for a daughter's locks or a son's arm; he leaves and "to no man did he speak of what he had seen or done".
2. **T** Sohrab: at one month "like a child of twelve", at ten no one resists him; asks his lineage; Tahmineh shows him
   Rostam's letter and gifts and begs silence ("Afrasiyab … would seek to destroy the son for hatred of the sire");
   Sohrab: "I will cast Kai Kaous from off his throne, I will give to Rustem the crown".
3. **T** Afrasiab's plan (to Human and Barman): "from Rustem must it be hidden who it is that goeth out against him …
   if Sohrab fall under Tehemten, the grief he shall endure … will bring him to the grave for sorrow."
4. **T** The White Castle: Hujir captured by Sohrab; Gurdafrid (Gordafarid) fights him in disguise and escapes.
5. **T** Rostam, in the garb of a Turk, spies in the night; Zindeh ("brother unto Tahmineh … she had sent him forth with
   her son that he might point out to him his father") challenges him; Rostam "struck him and laid him dead".
6. **T** Hujir is asked three times about the green tent; he says "an ally from far Cathay" and "I will not show him unto
   thee"; Sohrab kills him.
7. **T** The first meeting: "the air is warm and soft, but the earth is cold. I have pity upon thee"; "it seemeth unto me
   that thou art none other than Rustem" — "Thou errest, I am not Rustem … I am a slave"; Rostam says it "that Sohrab
   might be afraid … and deem that yet greater might was hidden in the camp of his enemy". Fight with spears, swords,
   clubs, hands, arrows; neither wins. Human later tells Sohrab this man is not Rostam, "because that Afrasiyab had
   enjoined him to lead Sohrab into destruction".
8. **T** Day 2: Sohrab: "let us doff our armour and seat ourselves together in amity … tell me thy name"; Rostam: "we are
   not come forth to parley but to combat". Evening: Sohrab throws him, draws his sword; Rostam: "he who overthroweth a
   brave man for the first time should not destroy him"; Sohrab lets him go; Human: "thou didst fall into a snare".
9. **T** Rostam prays at a brook; the rock gives way under his strength and he asks for part to be taken back; he throws
   Sohrab by the girdle and "broke his back like to a reed". Sohrab: "if thou shouldest become a fish that swimmeth in
   the depths of the ocean, if thou shouldest change into a star … my father would draw thee forth … my father is Rustem
   the Pehliva." "Open, I beseech thee, mine armour, and regard the jewel upon mine arm."
10. **T** Sohrab asks Rostam not to let the Shah fall on the men of Turan; Rostam sends word to Human to withdraw; Gudarz
    carries Rostam's plea for the balm; Kay Kavus "remembered only the proud words" and "was afraid lest the might of
    Sohrab be joined to that of his father"; a messenger tells Rostam Sohrab is dead.
11. **T** Rostam burns his tent, saddle, leopard-skin and armour; Sohrab buried in a tomb "like to a horse's hoof";
    Tahmineh "set fire unto the house of Sohrab … and when a year had thus rolled over her bitterness, the breath departed
    from out her body".

12. **T** Gordafarid (Gurdafrid): comes out in armour, fights Sohrab with arrows and a lance, "it wanted little and she would
    have thrown him from his seat"; he catches her and tears off her helmet - "coils of dusky hue"; "If the daughters of
    Iran are like to thee ... none can stand against this land"; she tells him the army saw them fight and will gibe;
    he follows her to the gate and she "shut the door upon him".
13. **T** Kay Kavus sends Gew with a letter ("if thou bearest roses in thy hands, stop not to smell them"). Rostam: "I
    should not marvel if such an hero arose in Iran ... I have myself a son in Samengan, but he is yet an infant", then feasts
    Gew for three days ("none on earth dare be angered with me"). Kavus: "hang him upon the nearest gallows"; Rostam: "I am a
    free man and no slave ... without Rustem Kai Kaous is as nothing"; he turns to leave Iran; Gudarz and the nobles bring
    him back; Kavus "stepped down from off his throne ... craved his pardon".

CAUSES
Pride (Rostam), concealment (Tahmineh), ambition (Sohrab), guile (Afrasiab), envy (Kay Kavus), loyalty (Hujir).

CONSEQUENCES
Rostam withdraws in grief; Turan retreats; the war ends without a battle of armies.

MOST INTERESTING FACTS
- The only man who could have named Rostam to Sohrab (Zindeh) is killed by Rostam the night before.
- Every plan to protect someone ends up protecting the secret: Hujir's lies, Afrasiab's order, Tahmineh's silence, Rostam's pride.
- Rostam offered the boy his life first ("I have pity upon thee").
- The final request of a dying son is a request for mercy for his army.

MYTHS / LEGENDS / TRADITIONS
All of it. The script frames it as a legend of the *Shahnameh*, told in the voice of the tradition. Parallels
(Hildebrandslied, the Irish *Aided Óenfhir Aífe*, the Indian Babruvahana) are in Wikipedia's "See also" — **not
in the script**; saved as a seed for the "Same Story, Different World" playlist.

UNCERTAINTIES / DEBATES
1. **Source is a retelling.** Zimmern (1883) is a prose retelling, not a line translation; wording and some details
   differ between retellings (Wikipedia: the token is "a jewel and a seal"; Zimmern: an onyx on the arm; others: an armband
   or amulet; Wikipedia says Rostam "stabs" Sohrab, Zimmern says he drew his sword). The script says "armband" and "sword" and names no jewel.
2. **Order and count of the duels:** 3 meetings across two days; the script says "the next morning".
3. **Zindeh's death and the three lies of Hujir** come from Zimmern; I did not verify them in a second translation
   (Warner, Clinton or Davis). They are in the Persian text as I know it, but this is **not confirmed in this session**.
4. **Date of the poem:** Wikipedia says "10th-century Persian epic"; the completion year 1010 is the standard date and
   was not re-checked in a primary page here.
5. Variant endings exist (a Tajik film and an Iranian animation make Sohrab survive); **not used**.

RECOMMENDED VOICE
Ancient / mysterious.

VOICE CHARACTER
Deep, deliberate, atmospheric, slightly dark; plain, not ornate; weight on the quiet turns.

VOICE DELIVERY
~143 wpm. A small beat after "his name" in the hook. Dialogue read simply, without character voices. A slow, low
"the air is warm and soft, but the earth is cold". "I am a slave" quick and cold. The armband line quiet. Kay Kavus's
refusal flat. The last two sentences very quiet.

WHY THIS VOICE FITS
A legend carried by recognition and silence. A commander voice would make the duel a fight; a dark voice would make the
tragedy melodrama. The ancient voice lets the plot do the work.
