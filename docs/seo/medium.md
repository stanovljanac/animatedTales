# How Did the Ancient Greeks Build a Computer 2,000 Years Ago?

*And nine more stories from history and myth, each told in about five minutes of animation*

In the spring of 1900, a boat of Greek sponge divers sheltered from a storm off Antikythera, a small rocky island between Crete and mainland Greece. When the sea calmed, one diver went down to look around. According to the story the crew told, he came back up terrified. The seabed, he said, was covered in bodies.

The bodies were statues, the cargo of a ship that had sunk more than two thousand years earlier. Along with the statues, glass and pottery, the divers raised a shapeless lump of corroded bronze that nobody paid much attention to. Two years later the lump split apart, and an archaeologist named Valerios Stais saw something that did not seem to belong in the ancient world: a gear wheel.

That lump is now known as the Antikythera mechanism. X-ray scans in 2005 found about thirty surviving gears and thousands of tiny letters hidden under the corrosion. You turned a handle on the side, and the machine moved the sky. It tracked the Sun and the Moon through the zodiac and predicted eclipses years in advance. Nothing of comparable complexity is known again for more than a thousand years.

This is the kind of story I make animated videos about on [Animated Tales](https://www.youtube.com/@AnimatedTales-r4r). You can watch this one here: [Divers Found a 2,000-Year-Old Greek Computer in a Shipwreck (4:06)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_eKElXW9Hk4).

## One story, a few minutes

Animated Tales is a YouTube channel with one rule: each video tells one story from history or mythology, from beginning to end, in about five minutes. It covers one turning point, one mystery or one myth, never the entire history of an empire.

It is made for people who like history and myths but do not always have an hour for a documentary. You can watch an episode on a coffee break and come away knowing a complete story: what happened, why it mattered, and what is still uncertain.

Three things make the format different:

- **One story, kept short.** Episodes run from 3:09 to 5:08. Nothing is added to reach a running time.
- **Myth is marked as myth.** Historical record, tradition and open debate are kept apart. When a story comes from legend, the narration says so.
- **Animation that carries the story.** The videos use hand-drawn-style 2D animation to show what narration alone cannot show quickly: how small the Antikythera fragment is in a hand, or how thin a magical ribbon looks next to the iron chains a giant wolf has already broken.

## Three stories to start with

### A war fought over horses

Han China wanted the horses of Dayuan, a kingdom in the Ferghana valley. Dayuan refused to sell them, so the emperor sent an army. The war that followed is often called the War of the Heavenly Horses. The first expedition went so badly that the emperor refused to let its survivors back through the Jade Gate.

It is a story about how something that sounds small, like horses, can decide what an empire is able to do.

[Watch: A Kingdom Refused to Sell Its Horses. So China Sent an Army (4:42)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NHMGOOVNNk0)

### The god who put his hand in a wolf's mouth

In Norse tradition, the gods feared the wolf Fenrir for what he would become. They bound him with an iron chain called Læding, and he broke it. They brought a stronger one called Drómi, and he broke that too.

So they turned to the dwarfs, who made Gleipnir: a binding as thin as a ribbon, made from six impossible things. The sound of a cat's footsteps, the roots of mountains, the sinews of bears, the breath of fish, the spittle of birds and the beard of a woman. Fenrir was suspicious of something that looked so weak. He agreed to be bound only if one of the gods placed a hand in his mouth as a guarantee.

Týr stepped forward.

[Watch: The God Who Sacrificed His Hand to Save the World (3:31)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MZ5I5s2hapA)

## How the episodes are made

How a video is made matters for how far you should trust it, so here is the process.

- **Story selection.** Ideas are scored on ten points before one is made, including the strength of the hook, whether the story has a real arc in five minutes, how surprising it is and how solid the history is. A person chooses which one gets made.
- **Research with labels.** Every claim is sorted into one of three groups: historical record (backed by documents or physical evidence), tradition (myth, legend and later storytelling) or debate (uncertain or disputed). In the Antikythera episode, the gear wheel noticed in 1902 is record, the diver's story of bodies on the seabed is tradition, and the date the machine was built, anywhere from about 205 to 87 BC, is debate. The script keeps those lines visible.
- **Narration first.** The narration is measured first, and the pictures are cut to fit it.
- **Period details.** Visual details are researched and designed to reflect the historical period.
- **Subtitles.** Every video has its own uploaded subtitles instead of automatic captions.

**About AI.** AI tools are part of the production. They help with research and script drafts, they voice the narrator, and they create the hand-drawn-style illustrations and animation. Every story is chosen and reviewed by a person before it is published. Animated Tales is made by one person. The six newest episodes go through the full process above; the first four were made before it existed.

These are stories, not lectures. Each one is a clear first look at a subject, not an academic source. If a story grabs you, the next step is the original texts: Herodotus for the Battle of Marathon and for Croesus, the Prose Edda for Týr and Fenrir.
